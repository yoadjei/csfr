\begin{abstract}
Image evidence recovered from damaged media often has contiguous regions missing, and an examiner who reconstructs them must be able to say how the result depends on the pixels that survived. CSFR (constraint-driven sparse fragment reconstruction) computes the unique minimiser of a strictly convex objective, a smoothed DCT sparsity term with total variation and a high-frequency penalty, with the observed pixels and the 8-bit range as hard constraints. Projected FISTA with adaptive restart reaches the minimiser, and a Frank--Wolfe gap certifies every output. Each output also carries a sensitivity record, the derivative of the reconstruction with respect to the observed pixels, whose accuracy is measured by re-solving perturbed problems. CSFR is compared with \methodsTotal\ methods, \methodsClassical\ classical and one learned, on three patch corpora with cluster-aware statistics, on byte-corrupted JPEG files decoded by a standard decoder, on full-resolution colour images and on two downstream tasks. \resultpending{rank and gap to the strongest classical method per family with intervals, including scattered loss, where block-DCT thresholding leads; gap to LaMa; record fidelity; JPEG; cost}. The certificate and the record make the computation checkable; they do not establish that a reconstruction is semantically correct. Code, data and the result files behind every table are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Image evidence is among the most frequently encountered artefact classes in digital forensics. Photographs, screenshots, document scans and CCTV stills are routinely recovered from unallocated space, slack space, swap files and damaged media~\cite{carrier2005forensics,casey2011digital,garfinkel2007carving}. Recovered images are often fragmented, partially overwritten or truncated, and reconstructing a usable image from such evidence comes before most downstream tasks: identification, optical character recognition and human review.

Three kinds of tool are available, and each falls short for an examiner in a different way. Signature-based carvers recover whole files but do not reconstruct damaged regions~\cite{pal2009evolution,garfinkel2007carving}. Deep generative inpainters produce convincing completions~\cite{yu2018generative,suvorov2022resolution,ho2020denoising,rombach2022high,lugmayr2022repaint}, but each imputed pixel is drawn from a prior learned on other images. It is therefore a plausible inference rather than a function of the recovered data alone~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations,horsman2024sources,arslan2024reliability}, which may raise questions under reliability standards for expert evidence~\cite{daubert1993}. Classical inpainting and interpolation use only the image under examination and are the natural comparison for an evidential tool. They answer a narrower question than an examiner asks, however: they return pixels, but no statement of how accurately the computation was carried out or of how each imputed value depends on the observed ones.

This paper asks whether a reconstruction can come with both statements, and what they cost in accuracy. CSFR (constraint-driven sparse fragment reconstruction) computes the unique minimiser of a strictly convex objective, with the observed pixels and the 8-bit range as hard constraints; the objective combines a smoothed DCT sparsity term, total variation and a high-frequency penalty. Every output carries a certificate that bounds its suboptimality with respect to that minimiser. It also carries a sensitivity record, the Jacobian of the solution map with respect to the observed pixels, whose accuracy is checked by solving perturbed problems again. The design deliberately gives up two things. The first is priors learned outside the image. The second is non-convex steps, such as iterated hard thresholding, which can be more accurate but admit neither a certificate nor a differentiable solution map.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A certified convex reconstruction.} The problem is stated exactly as it is solved, with every constant, and shown to have a unique minimiser (Section~\ref{sec:problem}). Projected FISTA with adaptive restart reaches it, and a Frank--Wolfe gap with a calibrated tolerance certifies every output (Section~\ref{sec:framework:solver}).
  \item \textbf{A validated sensitivity record.} The record is the derivative of the solution map, and its rows provably sum to one whenever no imputed pixel is at a bound of the range. Its accuracy is measured against re-solves at two tolerances (Sensitivity Record Fidelity), and it is compared with the exact record of linear interpolation and with finite-difference records of PDE inpainting (Sections~\ref{sec:framework:audit}, \ref{sec:results:fidelity} and~\ref{sec:results:records}).
  \item \textbf{A forensic-realism evaluation.} It covers JPEG files corrupted at the byte level and decoded by a standard decoder, with three ways of obtaining the mask; robustness to errors in the mask; and full-resolution colour images (Sections~\ref{sec:results:jpeg}, \ref{sec:results:maskerr} and~\ref{sec:results:fullres}).
  \item \textbf{A same-harness comparison with accuracy reported as measured.} CSFR is compared with \methodsTotal\ methods, \methodsClassical\ classical and one learned, on three patch corpora with cluster-aware statistics. The evaluation adds a constraint ablation, a comparison with a fixed-budget solver and two downstream tasks (Sections~\ref{sec:experiments} and~\ref{sec:results}). It reports where CSFR is not the most accurate method as plainly as where it is.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Sparse and variational inpainting.} Filling a hole from the rest of the image has both convex and non-convex solutions. Variational methods propagate structure inward from the boundary. Examples are PDE inpainting~\cite{bertalmio2000image}, total variation~\cite{rudin1992nonlinear} and the inpainting models of Chan and Shen~\cite{chan2002mathematical}, fast marching~\cite{telea2004image} and biharmonic interpolation~\cite{damelin2018surface}. Sparse methods fill the hole with a signal that is sparse in a transform or a learned dictionary:
\begin{itemize}
  \item morphological component analysis separates cartoon and texture layers using TV and a DCT dictionary~\cite{elad2005simultaneous};
  \item Guleryuz iterates hard thresholding of overlapping block DCTs~\cite{guleryuz2006nonlinear};
  \item frequency-selective reconstruction fits a sparse Fourier model block by block~\cite{seiler2015resampling};
  \item dictionary methods learn their atoms from the image itself~\cite{elad2006image,mairal2009online,aharon2006ksvd}.
\end{itemize}
Exemplar and shift-map methods copy content from elsewhere in the image~\cite{criminisi2004region,pritch2009shift}. CSFR belongs to the convex sparse-plus-TV family. Its objective is close to that of morphological component analysis, without the layer separation. This paper evaluates the families above under one harness (Section~\ref{sec:experiments:methods}) rather than ranking them from the literature.

\textbf{Compressed sensing.} Sparse signals can be recovered from few incoherent measurements by convex $\ell_{1}$ minimisation~\cite{candes2006robust,donoho2006compressed,candes2006stable}, with the restricted isometry property~\cite{candes2008restricted} and incoherence~\cite{candes2007sparsity} as sufficient conditions. These results motivate the sparsity term, but their conditions concern random measurements and cannot be checked for an individual mask. Section~\ref{sec:problem:oracle} replaces them with a per-mask measurement.

\textbf{Convergent first-order methods.} Problems of this kind are solved with convergence guarantees by accelerated proximal gradient~\cite{beck2009fast}, by primal--dual splitting for TV-type problems~\cite{chambolle2011first} and with adaptive restart~\cite{odonoghue2015adaptive}. The Frank--Wolfe gap~\cite{frank1956algorithm} bounds suboptimality over a compact set. CSFR uses these methods unchanged. What matters for evidence is that the solver runs to a certificate and that the certificate is released with every output. The reconstruction is then a function of the evidence, not of an iteration budget.

\textbf{Sensitivity of regularised solutions.} The derivative of the solution of a regularised inverse problem with respect to its observations has been studied for risk estimation and parameter selection. It gives the degrees of freedom of partly smooth regularisers~\cite{vaiter2017degrees}, and SUGAR differentiates it through iterative solvers~\cite{deledalle2014stein}. At a smooth minimiser, the implicit function theorem~\cite{dontchev2014implicit} gives it directly. CSFR uses the same object for a different purpose: as a per-pixel record of how the output depends on the evidence, validated against re-solves rather than assumed.

\textbf{Generative inpainting.} Generative methods sample the hole from a prior fitted to an external corpus. Examples are GAN completion~\cite{yu2018generative}, Fourier-convolutional large-mask inpainting~\cite{suvorov2022resolution}, denoising~\cite{ho2020denoising} and latent~\cite{rombach2022high} diffusion, RePaint~\cite{lugmayr2022repaint}, pluralistic completion~\cite{zheng2019pluralistic}, and latent- and gradient-guided inpainters~\cite{corneanu2024latentpaint,grechka2024gradpaint}. Learned restoration more broadly shares the dependence on weights fitted outside the fragment. This includes unfolded networks~\cite{le2024unfolded,mou2022deep}, compression-artefact reduction~\cite{ma2024sensitivity} and blind denoising~\cite{zhang2024scalable}. Such methods may be the better tool for triage and lead generation. When a reconstruction is offered as evidence, however, a learned prior prevents deriving each output pixel from the examined input. LaMa~\cite{suvorov2022resolution} is evaluated on this paper's corpora, so that the cost of the choice is measured rather than assumed.

\textbf{File carving.} Carving~\cite{garfinkel2007carving,pal2009evolution,poisel2013advanced,mikus2005evaluation} locates and orders fragments rather than reconstructing damaged content. This includes content-based fragment classification~\cite{calhoun2008sceadan} with entropy measures~\cite{shannon1948entropy}, and the recovery of JPEG fragments whose headers are missing~\cite{uzun2015carving}. Learned repair of bitstream-corrupted JPEGs works at the codec level, with a learned prior~\cite{liu2023bitstream}. CSFR complements a carver: it operates in the decoded pixel domain on the fragments that the carver has located.

\textbf{Reliability of forensic methods.} Daubert~\cite{daubert1993} and SWGDE guidance~\cite{swgde2023} emphasise testability, known error rates and documentation, and Farid~\cite{farid2009digital} surveys the legal context of image forensics. Regularised reconstruction can itself create features absent from the object, particularly in the null space of the measurement operator~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations,tivnan2024hallucination}, and the forensics literature has begun to formalise tool error and its disclosure~\cite{horsman2024sources,arslan2024reliability}. CSFR does not remove the null-space problem. Its certificate and record make the computation, and its dependence on the evidence, open to inspection; they do not certify that missing content is correct.

The components above are standard. This paper contributes their combination into a reconstruction tool whose every output carries an optimality certificate and a validated sensitivity record, together with a same-harness measurement of what that combination costs in accuracy.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Observation model and scope}
\label{sec:problem:scope}

A grey-level patch $X \in [0,255]^{H \times W}$ with $N = HW$ pixels is observed on a set $\Omega$ of positions, recorded as a binary mask $\mathbf{M}$ with $\mathbf{M}_{rc} = 1$ on $\Omega$. The observed values $Y_{\Omega} = X_{\Omega}$ are known exactly; the remaining pixels, the hole $\Omega^{c}$, are unknown. Observation is noiseless on $\Omega$ by assumption, because an observed pixel is evidence and is reproduced rather than denoised. What happens when $\Omega$ itself is wrong is measured in Section~\ref{sec:results:maskerr}.

\textbf{Domain of reconstruction.} CSFR operates on decoded pixel arrays and does not recover the encoded bitstream. Locating, ordering and decoding fragments is the work of the carver and decoder that precede it, and re-encoding a repaired file is outside the method. The experiments use two kinds of input: synthetic masks applied to decoded images (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), and JPEG files whose bytes were corrupted and then decoded by a standard decoder, with three ways of obtaining the mask (Section~\ref{sec:results:jpeg}). Every statement about the record refers to the pixel-domain map from $(Y_{\Omega}, \mathbf{M})$ to the output.

\subsection{Objective and constraints}
\label{sec:problem:objective}

Let $A = D_{H} X D_{W}^{\top}$ be the orthonormal two-dimensional DCT-II coefficients of $X$, where $D_{n}$ is the $n \times n$ DCT-II matrix, and let
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\varepsilon}(z) = \sqrt{z^{2} + \varepsilon^{2}} - \varepsilon
\end{equation}
be the pseudo-Huber penalty~\cite{charbonnier1997deterministic}, which is smooth, strictly convex, quadratic for $|z| \ll \varepsilon$ and close to $|z| - \varepsilon$ for $|z| \gg \varepsilon$. Forward differences $(\nabla_{v} X)_{rc} = X_{r+1,c} - X_{rc}$ and $(\nabla_{h} X)_{rc} = X_{r,c+1} - X_{rc}$ are taken only between pixels inside the patch (Neumann boundary); there are $N_{v} = (H-1)W$ and $N_{h} = H(W-1)$ of them. The high-frequency index set is $\mathcal{H} = \{(k,l) : k/(H-1) + l/(W-1) \ge \csfrHFcut\}$. CSFR computes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
X^{\star} = \arg\min_{X}\; J(X) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad X_{\Omega} = Y_{\Omega}, \quad 0 \le X \le 255,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:J}
\begin{aligned}
J(X) = {} & \frac{\lambda_{\ell_1}}{N} \sum_{(k,l) \neq (0,0)} \phi_{\varepsilon}(A_{kl})
\;+\; \frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{FC}}}{N} \sum_{(k,l) \in \mathcal{H}} A_{kl}^{2} \\
& +\; \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} \Bigl[ \frac{1}{N_{v}} \sum_{r,c} \phi_{\varepsilon}\bigl((\nabla_{v} X)_{rc}\bigr) + \frac{1}{N_{h}} \sum_{r,c} \phi_{\varepsilon}\bigl((\nabla_{h} X)_{rc}\bigr) \Bigr],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\ell_1} = \csfrLamLone$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} = \csfrLamTV$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{FC}} = \csfrLamFC$ and $\varepsilon = \csfrEps$ grey level for every patch, corpus and corruption family. Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} is the problem the released code solves; there is no separate idealised programme and no penalised surrogate. Four choices in it are deliberate.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Each term is a mean over its index set rather than a sum, so one set of weights applies to every patch size, including the full-resolution images of Section~\ref{sec:results:fullres}.
  \item The DC coefficient is excluded from the sparsity term. It carries the mean intensity, which is not sparse, and penalising it would pull the mean of the hole toward zero.
  \item There is no data-fidelity term. Fragment consistency is a constraint, not a penalty, so the observed pixels are never traded against smoothness.
  \item The weights are those of the released configuration (\texttt{configs/\allowbreak csfr\allowbreak\_sweep\allowbreak\_2d.yaml}). They were fixed before the GovDocs1 and Kodak corpora of this paper were assembled, and no weight was changed for any experiment reported here.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:constraints} maps the four constraint families of the method onto Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective}. C1 and C3 are hard constraints that every iterate satisfies; C2 and C4 are penalty terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:J}, so a reconstruction can have any amount of variation or high-frequency energy at a cost. For that reason constraint violations are never compared across methods, which would reward CSFR for the terms it optimises. The one violation figure reported is internal to the ablation: the fraction of imputed pixels that leave the range when C3 is removed (Section~\ref{sec:results:ablation}).

\label{sec:problem:c3}
C3 is the global dynamic range of an 8-bit image. Bounds derived from the observed neighbourhood of each pixel would be a stronger constraint, but they are not implemented or evaluated in this paper, and C3 should not be read as a local plausibility check.

\begin{proposition}[unique minimiser]
\label{prop:unique}
Let $\Omega \neq \emptyset$ and $\lambda_{\ell_1} > 0$ or $\lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} > 0$. For every patch $X$ and every set $F \subseteq \Omega^{c}$ of hole pixels the Hessian block $\nabla^{2}_{FF} J(X)$ is positive definite, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} has exactly one minimiser.
\end{proposition}
\textit{Proof.} Since $\phi_{\varepsilon}'' > 0$, each term of $J$ has a positive semidefinite Hessian. The DCT is orthonormal, so the Hessian of the sparsity term vanishes only on directions whose DCT is zero outside the DC coefficient, which are the constant patches; the Hessian of the TV term vanishes only on directions with zero differences, which on the connected pixel grid are again the constants. A constant direction supported on $F$ is zero, because it vanishes on $\Omega \neq \emptyset$. Hence $\nabla^{2}_{FF} J \succ 0$ and $J$ is strictly convex on the feasible set, which is non-empty, convex and compact, so the minimiser exists and is unique.~$\square$

The reconstruction is therefore a property of $(Y_{\Omega}, \mathbf{M})$ and the fixed constants. The initialisation, the solver and the iteration count affect only how closely it is approximated, and Section~\ref{sec:framework:solver} bounds that approximation for every patch.

\subsection{What the observed pixels can determine}
\label{sec:problem:oracle}

The formulation is motivated by sparse recovery: natural patches are approximately sparse in the DCT, and convex $\ell_{1}$ minimisation subject to the observations recovers a sparse signal from few incoherent samples~\cite{candes2006robust,donoho2006compressed}. The pixel and DCT bases are highly incoherent (scaled mutual coherence $\sqrt{N}\mu = \oracleCoherenceScaled$ on a scale from $1$ to $\sqrt{N}$~\cite{candes2007sparsity}), but the recovery results built on incoherence assume that the observed positions are drawn uniformly at random. That describes scattered loss and not bursts, overwrites or truncation, and no recovery condition of this kind can be verified for an individual mask.

We therefore replace a recovery bound with a measurement. For each patch, let $S$ be the positions of its $\kappa$ largest-magnitude DCT coefficients, with $\kappa$ equal to 5\% of the pixels, and let $\Phi_{S}$ be the corresponding basis images. The oracle reconstruction fits $\Phi_{S}$ to the observed pixels by least squares. The oracle knows the true support, which no method does. Its hole PSNR describes what a $\kappa$-sparse DCT model can recover from that mask when the support is known, and the condition number of the observed rows of $\Phi_{S}$ measures how well the observations determine the coefficients on that support. Both describe the difficulty of a mask; neither is a bound on any method. Section~\ref{sec:results:oracle} reports them per corruption family.

\section{Solver, Certificate and Sensitivity Record}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Projected accelerated gradient with restart}
\label{sec:framework:solver}

The gradient of $J$ is Lipschitz-continuous with constant
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lipschitz}
L = \frac{\lambda_{\ell_1}}{N\varepsilon} + \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} \Bigl( \frac{4}{N_{v}\varepsilon} + \frac{4}{N_{h}\varepsilon} \Bigr) + \frac{2\lambda_{\mathrm{FC}}}{N},
\end{equation}
because $\phi_{\varepsilon}'' \le 1/\varepsilon$, the DCT is orthonormal and each one-dimensional difference operator has squared norm at most four. The feasible set $\mathcal{C} = \{X : X_{\Omega} = Y_{\Omega},\ 0 \le X \le 255\}$ fixes the observed coordinates and boxes the others. Because $Y_{\Omega}$ lies in the box, its Euclidean projection is exact and acts coordinate by coordinate: clip to $[0,255]$, then reset the observed pixels to $Y_{\Omega}$.

CSFR minimises Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} by projected FISTA~\cite{beck2009fast} with step $1/L$ and the gradient-based adaptive restart of O'Donoghue and Cand\`{e}s~\cite{odonoghue2015adaptive} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:csfr}), in double precision, starting from the zero-filled composite. The projection is applied at every iteration, so every iterate satisfies C1 and C3 exactly. There is no terminal correction step, and the released output is an iterate of the method rather than a post-processed version of one. The gradient and the Hessian--vector products are evaluated in closed form. For images with a side above 128 pixels, the DCT is computed with one FFT per axis~\cite{makhoul1980fast}.

\textbf{Stopping rule and certificate.} Every \csfrCheckEvery\ iterations the solver evaluates the Frank--Wolfe gap~\cite{frank1956algorithm}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gap}
G(X) = \max_{Z \in \mathcal{C}} \langle \nabla J(X),\, X - Z \rangle = \sum_{i \in \Omega^{c}} g_{i}\,(X_{i} - z_{i}),
\end{equation}
where $g = \nabla J(X)$, $z_{i} = 255$ where $g_{i} < 0$ and $z_{i} = 0$ elsewhere. Convexity gives $J(X) - J(X^{\star}) \le \langle \nabla J(X), X - X^{\star} \rangle \le G(X)$, so $G$ bounds the suboptimality of the current iterate in objective units without knowledge of $X^{\star}$. A patch stops when $G \le \csfrTol$ and is then frozen. A patch that reaches \csfrMaxIter\ iterations is reported as not converged; it is never silently accepted. Each patch's certificate is written beside its output and copied into every record manifest. It contains the iterations, the converged flag, $G$, $G/J$, the final projected-step size and the objective, together with $L$, the step and the full configuration.

The tolerance was calibrated, not chosen. On \calibNcells\ cells of the DFRWS corpus (\calibCells; \calibNpatches\ patches each), every patch was first solved to a reference gap of $\recRefTol$, close to the double-precision floor, and then at each tolerance from $\calibGridLoose$ to $\calibGridTight$. The criterion compares each solve with the minimiser, not with the ground truth, so it fixes numerical accuracy and cannot tune reconstruction quality. The default is the loosest tolerance whose 99th-percentile per-patch maximum pixel error against the reference is below $0.01$ grey levels in every cell. That tolerance is $\csfrTol$: in the worst cell the 99th-percentile error is $\calibPctlErrMax$ grey levels and the mean hole PSNR is within $\calibPsnrDiffMax$\,dB of the reference. The next looser value, $\calibNextTol$, fails on \calibNextFailCells. No calibration solve reached the iteration cap, and at the default tolerance the median iteration count ranged from \calibItersMin\ to \calibItersMax\ across the cells.

Two independent checks agree with the reference solves. L-BFGS-B (SciPy) on the same objective and box reached them within $\calibLbfgsbMaxDiff$ grey levels on the first \calibLbfgsbN\ patches of each cell. Zero-filled and Navier--Stokes initialisations, each solved to $\calibInitTol$, agree within $\calibInitMaxDiff$ grey levels, as Proposition~\ref{prop:unique} requires.

Two simpler stopping rules were rejected on measurement. A relative gap $G/J$ cannot be met on flat patches, where the double-precision floor of $G$ is a large fraction of a small $J$. A projected-step rule, which stops when no pixel moves by more than a threshold, stops far from the optimum in large holes: there the gradient is close to zero until information has propagated in from the boundary. On CF3~L5, a threshold of $\calibRejStepThresh$ grey levels stopped at a mean hole PSNR of \calibRejStepPsnr\,dB, against \calibRejRefPsnr\,dB at the minimiser.

\textbf{Fixed-budget comparison arm.} The configuration of the previously released implementation (\texttt{src/\allowbreak csfr\allowbreak\_reconstruct\allowbreak\_2d.py}) is evaluated only as a comparison arm (Section~\ref{sec:results:solver}). It runs Adam with learning rate $2.0$~\cite{kingma2015adam} on a penalised form of the objective with a quadratic data term, for a budget of $600$ iterations, with C3 applied by clipping after each step and C1 once at the end. It carries no certificate. Runtime and memory of both solvers are reported in Section~\ref{sec:results:cost}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{CSFR solver, as implemented in \texttt{src/\allowbreak csfr\allowbreak\_solver.py} (function \texttt{solve}). The patches of a batch are solved independently, and a converged patch is frozen.}
\label{alg:csfr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require observed values $Y$; mask $\mathbf{M}$ ($\mathbf{M}=1$ on $\Omega$); $\lambda_{\ell_1}, \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{FC}}, \varepsilon$; tolerance $\tau$; cap $K$; check interval $c$
\Ensure $\hat{X} \in \mathcal{C}$ and its certificate
\State $L \gets$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:lipschitz}
\State $P(X) \gets \mathbf{M}\odot Y + (1-\mathbf{M})\odot\mathrm{clip}(X, 0, 255)$ \Comment{projection onto $\mathcal{C}$}
\State $X_{0} \gets P(\mathbf{M}\odot Y)$; \; $Z \gets X_{0}$; \; $t \gets 1$
\For{$k = 1, \ldots, K$}
  \State $X_{k} \gets P\bigl(Z - \nabla J(Z)/L\bigr)$
  \If{$\langle Z - X_{k},\, X_{k} - X_{k-1} \rangle > 0$}
    \State $t \gets 1$ \Comment{adaptive restart}
  \EndIf
  \State $t' \gets \bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 + 4t^{2}}\bigr)/2$
  \State $Z \gets X_{k} + \tfrac{t-1}{t'}\,(X_{k} - X_{k-1})$; \; $t \gets t'$
  \If{$k \bmod c = 0$ \textbf{or} $k = K$}
    \If{$G(X_{k}) \le \tau$} \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap}}
      \State \Return $X_{k}$ with a certificate marked converged
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $X_{K}$ with a certificate marked not converged
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Sensitivity record}
\label{sec:framework:audit}

At the minimiser, let $F = \{ i \in \Omega^{c} : \recBoundMargin < X^{\star}_{i} < 255 - \recBoundMargin \}$ be the free set of hole pixels not at a bound, and $B = \Omega^{c} \setminus F$. Stationarity $\nabla_{F} J(X^{\star}) = 0$ holds on $F$, and $Y_{\Omega}$ enters the problem only through $X_{\Omega} = Y_{\Omega}$. Where strict complementarity holds at the pixels of $B$, so that small perturbations do not change which pixels are at a bound, the implicit function theorem~\cite{dontchev2014implicit} gives the Jacobian of the solution map:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:record}
\frac{\partial X^{\star}_{F}}{\partial Y_{\Omega}} = -\,H_{FF}^{-1} H_{F\Omega}, \qquad H = \nabla^{2} J(X^{\star}).
\end{equation}
$H_{FF}$ is invertible by Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}. The derivative of a pixel in $B$ is zero, and an observed pixel has the identity row because C1 fixes it.

The record of an imputed pixel $i$ is row $i$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:record}. It is computed by solving $H_{FF}\, w = e_{i}$ with conjugate gradients~\cite{hestenes1952methods} and the closed-form Hessian--vector products, and then forming $-H_{\Omega F}\, w$. Columns, the response of every free pixel to one observed pixel, are computed in the same way; they are the transposed view of the same matrix, which a test checks. All record computations use double precision, a relative CG residual of $\recCGrtol$ with a cap of \recCGmax\ iterations, and no damping. Weights smaller than $\recSupport$ times the largest weight are not counted in the reported support. Units are grey levels of the reconstructed pixel per grey level of the observed pixel.

\begin{proposition}[affine record]
\label{prop:affine}
If no hole pixel is at a bound ($B = \emptyset$), every row of Eq.~\eqref{eq:record} sums to one.
\end{proposition}
\textit{Proof.} The sparsity term excludes the DC coefficient, $\mathcal{H}$ does not contain it, and TV acts on differences, so $J(X + c\mathbf{1}) = J(X)$ for every constant $c$ and $H\mathbf{1} = 0$. With $F = \Omega^{c}$, the rows of $H$ indexed by $F$ give $H_{FF}\mathbf{1}_{F} + H_{F\Omega}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega} = 0$, hence $-H_{FF}^{-1} H_{F\Omega}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega} = \mathbf{1}_{F}$.~$\square$

The record is therefore an affine combination of the observed pixels: shifting every observed value by $c$ shifts every free pixel by $c$. The barycentric weights of linear interpolation also sum to one, but the CSFR weights are dense and can be negative, so they must not be read as contribution shares. The proposition also gives every record row a free accuracy check. Its affine defect, $1 - \sum_{k} w_{k}$, is zero for the exact Jacobian, and every manifest reports it beside the CG residual. For the same reason the linear system is not damped. A damped system $(H_{FF} + \gamma I)\,w = e_{i}$ is a common safeguard for CG, but it is unnecessary here because $H_{FF}$ is positive definite, and for any $\gamma > 0$ it violates Proposition~\ref{prop:affine}.

\textbf{What the record is and is not.} The record is the derivative of the map from observations to reconstruction, evaluated at the released output. It states how the reconstructed value would change, to first order, if an observed pixel had a different value. It is not a decomposition of the pixel value, it does not say which observations caused the content, and it says nothing about whether the reconstructed value is close to the truth. A first-order statement can also fail for perturbations of practical size, which is why the record is validated.

\textbf{Validation by perturb-and-re-solve.} For a queried observed pixel $k$ and a perturbation $\delta$, the predicted column $c_{\mathrm{pred}} = \partial X^{\star}_{F} / \partial Y_{k}$ is compared with the measured response $c_{\mathrm{act}}(\delta) = \bigl(X^{\star}(Y + \delta e_{k}) - X^{\star}(Y)\bigr)_{F} / \delta$, obtained by solving the perturbed problem again. The sign of $\delta$ is reversed where $Y_{k} + \delta$ would leave the range. The relative error is $e(\delta) = \|c_{\mathrm{act}} - c_{\mathrm{pred}}\|_{2} / \|c_{\mathrm{act}}\|_{2}$ over $F$, and it is measured twice:
\begin{itemize}
  \item against re-solves at a gap of $\recRefTol$, which asks whether the record is the derivative of the solution map;
  \item against re-solves at the operational tolerance $\csfrTol$, which asks what an examiner who re-runs the tool would observe.
\end{itemize}
The \emph{Sensitivity Record Fidelity} (SRF) of a set of queries is the fraction with $e(1) \le 0.10$. It is reported for both references, together with the sign agreement on the \recTopK\ largest predicted entries. Column queries are observed pixels at Chebyshev distance 1, 3 and at least 8 from the hole. Row queries, which the manifests display, are imputed pixels at distance 1, 3 and at the deepest point of the hole (Section~\ref{sec:results:fidelity}). Each manifest records the observation hashes, the objective and solver configuration, the certificate and the record parameters. For each query it records the largest weights, the support, the CG iterations and residual, the affine defect and the time taken. \texttt{src/\allowbreak sensitivity.py} implements the record.

\textbf{Comparison records.} A record is not unique to CSFR, because classical interpolators are deterministic and some have exact records. Linear interpolation on the Delaunay triangulation of the observed pixels has an exact, sparse Jacobian: each imputed pixel is a barycentric combination of at most three observed pixels. Telea and Navier--Stokes inpainting are deterministic but have no closed-form Jacobian, so their sensitivity can be obtained only by finite differences, at the cost of one re-run per observed pixel. Section~\ref{sec:results:records} compares these records with that of CSFR in type, exactness, support, fidelity and cost. What the convex formulation adds is a record for a global, non-local reconstruction, together with a certificate that the released output is the minimiser at which the record is evaluated.

\subsection{Warnings}
\label{sec:framework:warnings}

The implementation issues two warnings and states one limitation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A patch whose solve reaches the iteration cap is released with a not-converged flag, and its certificate says so.
  \item The Bounded-Unknown diagnostic is the isolated-missing fraction of the mask, the share of hole pixels with no observed four-neighbour. It is computed from the mask alone (\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak run\allowbreak\_downstream.py}), and a patch where it exceeds one half is labelled Bounded-Unknown for review. The label does not withhold the numerical output. Whether the statistic predicts reconstruction failure well enough to act on is tested against a rule fixed before the data were seen (Section~\ref{sec:results:bu}).
  \item The method does not certify that reconstructed content is correct. The certificate concerns the optimisation and the record concerns the solution map, so both should be treated as aids to examination, not as proof of recoverability.
\end{enumerate}
No stage uses a learned prior trained on data from outside the patch under examination.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Corpora}
\label{sec:experiments:corpora}

Three corpora of grey-level $64 \times 64$ patches carry the quantitative comparison. Further sets carry the forensic, full-resolution and downstream experiments.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{GovDocs1 (primary).} \corpusGovN\ patches from \corpusGovImages\ JPEG images of the GovDocs1 public corpus~\cite{garfinkel2009bringing}; no image contributes more than \corpusGovMaxShare\% of the patches.
  \item \textbf{DFRWS 2006 (secondary, forensic).} \corpusDfrwsN\ patches carved from the DFRWS 2006 challenge disk image. They come from \corpusDfrwsImages\ source images, the two largest of which contribute \corpusDfrwsTopTwoShare\% of the patches, so the corpus is analysed with cluster-aware intervals and not as independent units.
  \item \textbf{Kodak (tertiary, photographic).} \corpusKodakN\ patches from the \corpusKodakImages\ images of the Kodak set; no image contributes more than \corpusKodakMaxShare\%.
  \item \textbf{Full-resolution colour images.} Kodak images, colour JPEGs carved from the Nick Mikus file-system test images~\cite{mikus2005evaluation}, and GovDocs1 pages (Section~\ref{sec:results:fullres}).
  \item \textbf{Byte-corrupted JPEGs.} Baseline-sequential GovDocs1 JPEGs and the Mikus JPEG byte ranges, kept as original bytes and corrupted at the byte level (Section~\ref{sec:results:jpeg}).
  \item \textbf{Downstream sets.} SVHN digits for classification, and text-line crops of GovDocs1 pages for optical character recognition (Section~\ref{sec:results:downstream}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Patch extraction.} All patch corpora are built by \texttt{scripts/\allowbreak extract\allowbreak\_patches.py} under one seeded protocol. JPEG byte ranges are carved from the source by signature and decoded, and an image is kept if its shorter side is at least 96 pixels. Each kept image is divided into a non-overlapping grid with stride equal to the patch size, so no two patches share a pixel. Patches whose intensity standard deviation is below 10 grey levels are discarded, because PSNR is degenerate on flat content. The retained patches are sampled without replacement under a fixed seed, with a cap on the number of patches per image. The released manifest records every patch's source image, its coordinates, and the SHA-256 of the carved byte range.

\paragraph{Clustering.} Patches from one image share content, capture conditions and quantisation. Every analysis therefore reports intervals that resample source images as well as patches (Section~\ref{sec:experiments:stats}). The primary corpus was assembled with many images and few patches per image so that the two kinds of interval agree, and DFRWS 2006 is kept as constituted because it is the forensic corpus of record.

CF3 and CF4 erase a fixed band of rows and draw nothing at random, so their \sweepSeeds\ seeds share one mask. \subsection{Methods}
\label{sec:experiments:methods}

CSFR is compared with \methodsTotal\ methods under one harness, with the same patches, masks, seeds and metrics. Every output is retained, so every comparison is paired. The harness composites the observed pixels back into every output, so every method reproduces them exactly and the comparisons concern only what each method imputes.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Zero-fill} sets the hole to zero. It is reported as the floor and is not counted as a baseline.
  \item \textbf{Linear interpolation} on the Delaunay triangulation of the observed pixels. Pixels outside the convex hull of the observed pixels are set to zero, as in the released baseline. Under CF3 and CF4 the whole hole lies outside the hull, so the method reproduces the floor there and has no record.
  \item \textbf{Telea}~\cite{telea2004image} and \textbf{Navier--Stokes}~\cite{bertalmio2000image} inpainting, via OpenCV with radius $3$.
  \item \textbf{Dictionary learning}~\cite{mairal2009online,aharon2006ksvd}: a dictionary of $64$ atoms of $8 \times 8$ pixels is learned from the fully observed tiles of each image, and each incomplete tile is coded on its observed pixels with $8$ non-zero coefficients. The dictionary is fitted by the online algorithm of scikit-learn with the settings of the released baseline: $30$ passes in batches of $32$ tiles, with the coordinate-descent sparse coding of each step capped at $200$ iterations. The cap is often reached. With a cap \dictlearnCapFactor\ times larger, the mean hole PSNR of a cell changed by at most \dictlearnCapMaxCellShift\,dB, and that of a single patch by at most \dictlearnCapMaxPatchShift\,dB, on the first \dictlearnCapPatches\ patches of each of the \dictlearnCapCells\ cells of CF2 to CF4 at mask seed~0 of the primary corpus. With fewer intact tiles than atoms, the method fills the hole with the observed mean. Under scattered loss few tiles are intact: on the primary corpus the fallback took place in \fallbackDictCFoneMin\% to \fallbackDictCFoneMax\% of the CF1 patch-runs, so the method's CF1 results are those of the mean fill.
  \item \textbf{Biharmonic} inpainting~\cite{damelin2018surface}, as implemented in scikit-image.
  \item \textbf{Iterated block-DCT thresholding} after Guleryuz~\cite{guleryuz2006nonlinear}: $8 \times 8$ DCT blocks on $16$ grid shifts, with the hard threshold decreasing geometrically from $128$ to $2$ over $100$ iterations and the hole initialised at the observed mean.
  \item \textbf{Frequency-selective reconstruction}~\cite{seiler2015resampling} in its fast mode and \textbf{shift-map} inpainting~\cite{pritch2009shift}, via OpenCV-contrib.
  \item \textbf{Exemplar-based inpainting} after Criminisi et al.~\cite{criminisi2004region}, with $9 \times 9$ patches, reduced to $7$, $5$ and $3$ when no fully observed exemplar exists. If none exists at size $3$, as under severe scattered loss, the remaining hole is filled with the observed mean and the patch is counted as a fallback; on the primary corpus that happened in \fallbackExemplarCFoneLfive\% of the CF1 L5 patch-runs. The quality tables mark every cell where a method fell back in most of its patch-runs.
  \item \textbf{TV-only}: the objective of Eq.~\eqref{eq:J} with $\lambda_{\ell_1} = \lambda_{\mathrm{FC}} = 0$, solved to the same certificate. It is both a baseline and an ablation arm.
  \item \textbf{LaMa}~\cite{suvorov2022resolution}, the external-prior method. It uses the released big-lama checkpoint through the simple-lama-inpainting package, with no fine-tuning, and runs at the native resolution of the input. The grey patch is replicated to three channels, and the output is composited onto the observed pixels. A single forward pass makes it deterministic. LaMa was trained on images much larger than $64 \times 64$, so the patch corpora lie outside its training distribution; the full-resolution experiment runs it in distribution (Section~\ref{sec:results:fullres}).
\end{enumerate}
The constraint ablation removes one part of CSFR at a time. Besides TV-only, it runs DCT-only ($\lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} = 0$), no high-frequency penalty ($\lambda_{\mathrm{FC}} = 0$) and no range constraint (C3 removed), at mask seed \sweepAblationSeeds\ of the primary corpus, for \methodsAblation\ arms in all. The fixed-budget Adam arm of Section~\ref{sec:framework:solver} runs on DFRWS 2006 only (Section~\ref{sec:results:solver}).

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:experiments:metrics}

Quality is scored over the hole only. Every method reproduces the observed pixels, so scoring them would add the same near-zero error to every method.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hole PSNR}: the mean squared error over $\Omega^{c}$ with peak 255, capped at 100\,dB. A patch reconstructed exactly, or to within the cap, enters a mean at 100\,dB. In a cell of $n$ patches with mean $\bar{p}$, it raises the mean by $(100 - \bar{p})/n$\,dB more than a patch at the mean would, which is a quarter of a decibel for $n = 300$ and $\bar{p} = 25$\,dB. Methods differ in how often they reach the cap. Every quality table therefore reports the number of patch-runs at the cap, and every hole-PSNR comparison is repeated without them (Section~\ref{sec:experiments:stats}).
  \item \textbf{Hole SSIM}~\cite{wang2004image}: the mean of the SSIM map over $\Omega^{c}$, with window 7 and data range 255.
  \item \textbf{GMSD}~\cite{xue2014gradient}: the standard deviation over $\Omega^{c}$ of the gradient-magnitude similarity map (Prewitt gradients, $c = 170$). It measures edge fidelity on the structured masks; lower is better.
\end{itemize}
Whole-patch PSNR is kept in the released files but not tabulated. The Hallucination Risk Proxy (HRP) is the mean absolute deviation of the imputed pixels from the mean of the observed region, in units of its standard deviation (floored at one grey level). The supplement reports it as a descriptive statistic only: it flags imputations outside the intensity distribution of the fragment, but cannot detect wrong values inside it. Constraint-violation rates are not compared across methods, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:problem:objective}. The record is scored by SRF (Section~\ref{sec:framework:audit}), and the downstream tasks by their own measures (Section~\ref{sec:results:downstream}).

\subsection{Statistics}
\label{sec:experiments:stats}

The analysis plan is written to the results directory before any statistic is computed (\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak run\allowbreak\_stats\allowbreak\_v2.py}). The unit is the patch. Each per-patch metric is averaged over the mask seeds before pairing, and differences are signed so that a positive value favours CSFR for every metric, GMSD included. For CSFR against each method, in each cell and for each metric, we report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the mean difference;
  \item a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}, with Holm adjustment~\cite{holm1979simple} across all comparisons of that metric;
  \item Cohen's $d_{z}$~\cite{cohen1988statistical} and Cliff's $\delta$~\cite{cliff1993dominance};
  \item 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals with \statsBoot\ resamples, resampling patches and, separately, source images, which respects the clustering.
\end{itemize}
Per source image, we report the mean difference, the fraction of images in which CSFR leads, and an image-level interval and test. The strongest competitor in a cell is the method with the best mean on the metric in question. It is identified after the fact, so the comparison with it is labelled as such and is not a pre-specified test.

\paragraph{Cap sensitivity.} Every hole-PSNR comparison, and the choice of the strongest competitor, is repeated on the cap-free subset of each cell: the patches on which no method, the floor included, reaches the 100\,dB cap at any seed. The primary analysis is unchanged. This sensitivity analysis was added after the first seed of the secondary corpus showed that methods differ in how often they reach the cap, and before the primary corpus was run.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:protocol}

CSFR, the classical methods and the analyses ran on one Windows~11 workstation with \envCores\ physical cores and no GPU, under Python~\envPython, PyTorch~\envTorch\ and OpenCV~\envOpencv. LaMa and the OCR engine ran in separate environments on the same machine. Every results directory records its interpreter, platform and package versions, together with the SHA-256 of the patch array it consumed, and those files are the authoritative record. The outputs of the fixed-budget Adam arm are the only artefacts from another environment: they come from the previously released sweep on an NVIDIA T4 under Linux and are used only in the solver comparison.

\paragraph{Determinism.} CSFR has no stochastic element, and the patches of a batch are solved independently. The classical methods run in single-thread worker processes, so their outputs do not depend on how the work is parallelised. Testing found one exception to determinism: the OpenCV-contrib shift-map implementation is not a function of its input. Its output depends on what the process computed before, and it differs between fresh processes. It therefore runs in a fresh interpreter for every call, so no shift-map output depends on the order of the work. Across \shiftmapRealisations\ realisations of every cell at mask seed~0 of the primary and the forensic corpus, up to \shiftmapDifferMax\% of the patches of a cell differed between realisations, and a cell mean moved by at most \shiftmapCellRangeMax\,dB; the shift-map results carry that additional uncertainty. The seed governs only mask generation and the fit of the dictionary-learning baseline. Bit-for-bit agreement across hardware and library versions is not claimed, because floating-point reduction order differs between builds. What carries across machines is the certificate: a re-run anywhere reaches the same unique minimiser to the accuracy measured in Section~\ref{sec:framework:solver}.

\subsection{Constraint ablation}
\label{sec:results:ablation}

Each arm removes one part of CSFR and is solved to the same certificate (Section~\ref{sec:experiments:methods}). Table~\ref{tab:ablation-v2} gives the hole-PSNR difference between CSFR and each arm on the primary corpus at mask seed \sweepAblationSeeds, and the share of imputed pixels that leave the range when C3 is removed.

Total variation carries the reconstruction. Without it (DCT only), hole PSNR is lower in every cell, by \ablNoTvMin\ to \ablNoTvMax\,dB, with a Holm-adjusted $p < 0.05$ in every cell.

The DCT and high-frequency terms are what CSFR adds to its TV-only arm, and on mean hole PSNR they cost more often than they gain: the TV-only arm is ahead in \ablTvNBehind\ of the 20 cells, by up to \ablTvBehindMax\,dB, and behind in \ablTvNAhead, by up to \ablTvAheadMax\,dB. Under burst and contiguous loss the difference has the cause found in Section~\ref{sec:results:quality}. Of the holes whose true content is flat, the TV-only arm reproduces \ablTvBcFlatCapShare\% exactly, and in every CF2 to CF4 cell those holes move the mean difference in its favour, by \ablTvBcFlatMin\ to \ablTvBcFlatMax\,dB. On the other holes CSFR is ahead in every such cell, by \ablTvBcRestMin\ to \ablTvBcRestMax\,dB; the median difference favours CSFR in every such cell, and CSFR is ahead on \ablTvBcAheadMin\% to \ablTvBcAheadMax\% of the patches. Scattered loss leaves no flat hole. There the TV-only arm is ahead at \ablTvScatterBehindLevels\ on most patches, with CSFR ahead on at most \ablTvScatterAheadShareMax\% of them, and CSFR is ahead at \ablTvScatterAheadLevels.

The high-frequency penalty has a cost under light scattered loss. Without it hole PSNR is higher by \ablNoFcScatterBehindMin\ to \ablNoFcScatterBehindMax\,dB at CF1 \ablNoFcScatterBehindLevels, where CSFR is ahead on at most \ablNoFcScatterAheadShareMax\% of the patches; it is lower at \ablNoFcScatterAheadLevels, by up to \ablNoFcScatterAheadMax\,dB, and the difference at \ablNoFcScatterNsLevels\ is not significant. Under burst and contiguous loss the flat holes favour the arm without the penalty in every cell, and on the other holes the penalty changes hole PSNR by at most \ablNoFcBcRestMaxAbs\,dB in either direction.

Removing the range constraint changes hole PSNR by at most \ablClipMaxAbs\,dB in any cell, but \ablClipShare\% of the imputed pixels then leave $[0, 255]$, by up to \ablClipMaxExcess\ grey levels; with C3 none do. C3 keeps the output an 8-bit image; it is not a source of accuracy.

\subsection{Byte-corrupted JPEG files}
\label{sec:results:jpeg}

Baseline-sequential JPEG files are corrupted in their entropy-coded scan, as damage on a storage medium would corrupt them: the file is truncated, a run of foreign bytes overwrites part of the scan, or one byte is flipped. A standard decoder in tolerant mode turns each damaged file into an image. It fills an unreadable tail with mid-grey, and after a corrupted run the Huffman decoding loses synchronisation, so the blocks that follow carry garbage. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What an Algorithm Can and Cannot Establish}
\label{sec:discussion:admissibility}

Admissibility is a determination made by a court, under a particular jurisdiction's rules, about a particular item of evidence in a particular case. An algorithm cannot possess this property, and no result in this paper claims that CSFR outputs are admissible. The reliability factors enumerated in \emph{Daubert}~\cite{daubert1993}, such as a known error rate, testability, peer review, and governing standards, describe the questions a court may ask. What a method can do is make those questions answerable.

On that narrower claim, CSFR makes four things checkable. The released output reproduces the observed pixels exactly. It carries a certificate that bounds its suboptimality with respect to the unique minimiser of a stated objective (Section~\ref{sec:framework:solver}), so the reconstruction is a function of the evidence and the objective rather than of an iteration budget or a stopping heuristic, and a re-run on another machine reaches the same minimiser to the measured accuracy. It can carry a sensitivity record whose agreement with re-solved problems has been measured (Section~\ref{sec:results:fidelity}). And the error of the method is measured on several corpora, with intervals that respect their clustering (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Each of these is a precondition for scrutiny, not a substitute for it. None of them determines admissibility or semantic correctness, and acceptance in the relevant scientific community is, for a method introduced in this paper, not yet established.

Generative inpainters occupy a different position on the same axes. For a sample from a learned prior, no closed-form statement of how the output depends on the input is generally available, and reproducibility depends on model weights and sampling state that the examining laboratory may not control or be able to disclose. These are real obstacles to evidential use. They are not obstacles to every forensic use of such tools.

\subsection{Imputed Content in Investigative, Analytical and Evidential Use}

The consequences of imputed content depend on what the reconstruction is used for, and collapsing that distinction overstates the case. Three uses can be separated.

In investigative triage, an examiner may use a visually plausible completion to identify fragments for further review. The output is a pointer, not evidence. Generative inpainting may be suitable if its outputs are labelled as generated and kept separate from the evidence record. In analytical use, a reconstruction can guide interpretation without being tendered, but imputed content may bias the examiner. Disclosure and separation of the source and reconstruction can reduce this risk. In evidential use, imputed content presents a more serious problem. A convincing but invented licence-plate digit changes the record from observation to inference. An opposing expert may then argue that the disputed character came from a training distribution rather than the examined disk. Separation matters for a further reason: downstream image-forensic detectors such as camera-model fingerprinting~\cite{cozzolino2020noiseprint} and forgery localisation~\cite{zhuo2022selfadversarial,wu2022robust} operate on pixel-level statistics that any imputation, learned or classical, alters, so such analyses should be run on the source rather than on a reconstructed derivative.

CSFR is aimed at analytical and evidential use. It uses no prior learned outside the fragment, but that does not make an imputed pixel correct: a value determined entirely by the observed pixels can still be wrong. The oracle-support diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:results:oracle} measures, per family, how much of the hole a sparse DCT model could recover from the observed pixels even with the true support known. Where it is low, no method that uses only the fragment can be expected to be accurate, and a record that is accurate as a derivative says nothing about the truth. Source evidence and reconstructed derivatives should therefore be kept separate.

\subsection{What the Certificate and the Record Add}

A record is not unique to CSFR. Linear interpolation has an exact and sparse one, and Telea and Navier--Stokes inpainting have finite-difference records at the cost of one re-run per observed pixel (Section~\ref{sec:results:records}). What the convex formulation adds is a record for a global reconstruction, obtained in closed form at a certified minimiser at the cost of one linear system per row (Section~\ref{sec:results:cost}), and validated against re-solves rather than assumed. Comparable statements about a generative model require interpretability techniques whose own validity is contested~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations,horsman2024sources}. The study therefore establishes the availability and the measured fidelity of the CSFR record, not exclusive traceability of the method.

The certificate matters for a reason the solver comparison makes concrete (Section~\ref{sec:results:solver}). A fixed-budget solver returns whatever iterate its budget reaches, so its output depends on the budget, the step size and the hardware as well as on the evidence, and a record evaluated there is not the derivative of a defined solution map. \resultpending{what the fixed budget cost the released numbers, and how far its outputs were from the minimiser}

\subsection{The Cost of Convexity}
\label{sec:discussion:convexity}

The design gives up non-convex steps, and the comparison measures what that costs. \resultpending{the gap to block-DCT thresholding under scattered loss and to the strongest method of each family} Iterated hard thresholding approximates a count of non-zero coefficients and re-selects its support at every threshold, whereas the $\ell_1$ term shrinks large coefficients as well as small ones. That shrinkage is one explanation consistent with a deficit where the observations determine the coefficients well, but the ablation does not isolate it. A non-convex method can be more accurate and can still be run deterministically. It has, however, neither a certificate of optimality nor a differentiable solution map at which a record is defined. That trade is the choice this paper makes, and the tables report its price.

\subsection{Operational Recommendation}
\label{sec:discussion:recommendations}

A cautious deployment would preserve the source image, store the reconstruction as a separate derivative, attach the certificate, the configuration and the records of the pixels in question, and require human review when the Bounded-Unknown label is raised and for any reconstruction offered as evidence. An output flagged as not converged is not the minimiser and should not be treated as one. \resultpending{what the downstream tasks imply for an examiner who reads a reconstruction} These findings do not establish that CSFR should replace other methods in operational casework.

\subsection{Adoption Scope and Validation Priorities}
\label{sec:discussion:scope}

CSFR is not proposed as a replacement for inpainting in general. It is for workflows that require deterministic processing, exact preservation of the observations, a certificate of the computation and an inspectable record of how imputed pixels depend on observed ones, and that can accept its measured accuracy. Where accuracy matters more than a checkable computation, as in investigative triage, the more accurate methods of Section~\ref{sec:results:quality} may be preferable, provided their outputs are labelled as reconstructions and kept apart from the source evidence.

The auditability claim is narrower than a claim of operational benefit. The fidelity of the record is measured across perturbation sizes and at two re-solve tolerances; whether examiners decide better when given it has not been studied. Direct comparison with SHAP, saliency methods and influence functions also remains open. Those methods were developed to explain predictive models, whereas the record is the derivative of a reconstruction with respect to its observations. Uncertainty quantification for learned reconstruction~\cite{edupuganti2021uncertainty,narnhofer2022bayesian,ekmekci2022uncertainty} addresses a related but distinct question, the dispersion of outputs under a learned prior, and offers protocols that future comparisons could adapt. Such comparisons should evaluate repeatability, faithfulness to controlled perturbations and examiner usefulness rather than visual similarity alone.

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

This section gathers the limits of what the evidence supports. Elsewhere, each limit is stated only where it applies.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Domain.} CSFR reconstructs decoded pixel arrays given a mask. It does not locate, order or decode fragments, repair the encoded bitstream or re-encode a file. The byte-corruption experiment shows how CSFR behaves behind a standard decoder (Section~\ref{sec:results:jpeg}). Storage-level fragmentation across non-contiguous clusters, progressive JPEGs and formats other than JPEG are untested.
  \item \textbf{Masks.} The quantitative comparison uses synthetic masks in four families. Real damage can mix the families, and a mask supplied by a carver or decoder is imperfect. Section~\ref{sec:results:maskerr} measures the effect of mask errors, but only for the error types simulated there.
  \item \textbf{Corpora.} The evaluation covers documents, a forensic disk image, photographs, full-resolution colour images and byte-corrupted JPEGs. Medical, satellite, low-light surveillance and screen-capture content are not measured. DFRWS 2006 is concentrated in few source images, so its results carry cluster-aware intervals and are secondary to those of the primary corpus.
  \item \textbf{One objective, fixed constants.} One objective is evaluated, with fixed weights and a smoothing of $\varepsilon = \csfrEps$ grey level, unchanged across corpora. Other weights would change the reconstructions, the records and possibly the ranking. Tuning per corpus was deliberately avoided, because a reconstruction whose parameters depend on the evidence is harder to defend. Because of the smoothing, the record is the derivative of the smoothed problem, and its fidelity is measured against that problem.
  \item \textbf{Accuracy.} Accuracy is reported as measured (Section~\ref{sec:results}). CSFR is not the most accurate method in every regime, and the paper does not claim that it is. It is offered for workflows that value the certificate and the record, at the measured cost.
  \item \textbf{Baselines.} The classical methods run at the fixed settings of Section~\ref{sec:experiments:methods}, taken from the published method, the library default or the released baseline, and tuning them per family could move the gaps in either direction. The shift-map implementation is not a function of its input, and its results carry the run-to-run variation reported in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. LaMa is used off the shelf; a learned method fine-tuned on forensic content was not evaluated.
  \item \textbf{The record.} The record is a first-order statement about the solution map. Its fidelity is measured for perturbations from \recDeltaMin\ to \recDeltaMax\ grey levels (Section~\ref{sec:results:fidelity}). Larger changes in the evidence can change the reconstruction non-linearly. Whether examiners decide better when given the record has not been studied.
  \item \textbf{Downstream tasks.} Digit classification and OCR on document text lines are evaluated; other recognition tasks are not.
  \item \textbf{Cost.} CSFR solves an optimisation problem per image and a linear system per record query. It is a tool for offline analysis, not for real-time triage (Section~\ref{sec:results:cost}).
  \item \textbf{Admissibility.} Whether a reconstruction is admissible is for a court to determine under its own rules. The certificate and the record make questions about the method answerable; they do not answer them.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Forensic reconstruction needs a clear line between observed and imputed pixels, processing that can be repeated, and explicit limits on what the output shows. CSFR draws that line in the decoded pixel domain. It computes the unique minimiser of a stated convex objective, with the observations and the 8-bit range as hard constraints, certifies every output by a Frank--Wolfe gap, and attaches a sensitivity record, the derivative of the solution map, whose accuracy is measured against re-solves rather than assumed.

The evaluation reports accuracy as measured, against \methodsTotal\ methods under one harness, with intervals that resample source images. \resultpending{the accuracy record: rank per family, the gap to block-DCT thresholding under scattered loss, the gap to LaMa, the byte-corrupted JPEG result} \resultpending{the fidelity of the record and its cost}

The evidence therefore supports CSFR where a checkable computation is required and its measured accuracy is acceptable, not as a general-purpose quality leader. The certificate and the record make the computation, and its dependence on the evidence, open to inspection. They do not establish that a reconstruction is semantically correct, and the method does not recover files at the byte level. Future work should test storage-level fragment alignment, compare the record with attribution methods under controlled perturbations, and study whether examiners use the record to better effect. The solver, the analyses and every result file behind the tables are released with the paper.
