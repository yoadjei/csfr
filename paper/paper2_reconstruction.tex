\begin{abstract}
Digital forensic analysis often encounters image evidence that is fragmented, corrupted or partially overwritten. Deep-learning inpainting can produce visually plausible completions, but pixels generated from an external learned prior cannot be attributed solely to the recovered input. We present Compressed Sensing Fragment Reconstruction (CSFR), a deterministic, training-free method for decoded pixel-domain reconstruction. CSFR minimises a penalised objective with fragment consistency, total-variation regularisation, global intensity clipping and frequency regularisation in a Discrete Cosine Transform basis. The associated idealised compressed-sensing estimator admits a conditional recovery bound under appropriate Restricted Isometry Property assumptions; the fixed-budget implementation does not provide a per-instance recovery certificate. We compare CSFR with six same-harness baselines over four corruption families, five severity levels and three seeds using 300 patches per cell. The evaluation includes a constraint ablation, paired and cluster-aware inference, iteration-budget sensitivity, external validation on 176 GovDocs1 source images and downstream Street View House Numbers digit classification. CSFR leads the strongest classical baseline by $1.0$ to $1.4$\,dB peak signal-to-noise ratio under random pixel loss, and this pattern reproduces on GovDocs1. The external-prior inpainter LaMa attains the highest raw quality on most structured-corruption cells but relies on a learned prior. Released CSFR outputs preserve observed pixels exactly and include an approximate local input-sensitivity record for sampled imputed pixels. These properties support reproducibility and audit, but do not establish semantic correctness or legal admissibility. Code, data and per-seed artefacts are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Image evidence is one of the most frequently encountered and most evidentially significant artefact classes in digital forensics. Photographs, screenshots, license-plate captures, document scans, and CCTV stills are recovered routinely from unallocated disk regions, slack space, swap files, and damaged storage media~\cite{carrier2005forensics,casey2011digital,garfinkel2007carving}. Unlike intact files retrieved through filesystem traversal, recovered image evidence is frequently \emph{fragmented} (split across non-contiguous clusters), \emph{corrupted} (bit-rot, burst damage, partial overwrite), or \emph{truncated} (interrupted write cycles, partial cluster allocation). Reconstructing a usable image from such evidence is therefore a precondition to almost every downstream forensic task: visual identification, optical character recognition, metadata extraction, and human review.

\subsection{The Deep-Learning Reconstruction Problem}

Image reconstruction has been transformed by deep generative models. GAN-based inpainting~\cite{yu2018generative}, large-mask Fourier-convolutional networks~\cite{suvorov2022resolution}, denoising-prior-driven restoration networks~\cite{dong2019denoising}, denoising diffusion probabilistic models~\cite{ho2020denoising}, latent diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high}, and diffusion-based inpainting variants~\cite{lugmayr2022repaint,zheng2019pluralistic} now produce reconstructions whose perceptual quality is, in many regimes, difficult for human observers to distinguish from the ground truth. These methods are powerful precisely because they replace the missing portion of an image with content drawn from a learned distribution over natural images.

In forensic work, this strength can become a liability. A pixel produced by a DL inpainter is sampled from the model's prior rather than derived solely from the recovered fragment bytes. The pixel is therefore a plausible inference about the missing content, not a verifiable statement of what the fragment contains. Recent analyses identify this distinction as a major obstacle to using generative models in forensic pipelines~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations,horsman2024sources,arslan2024reliability}. The reconstructed content cannot readily be traced to a closed algebraic transformation of the input. This limitation may also raise questions about reliability under \emph{Daubert}~\cite{daubert1993} and support challenges to the method used in an investigation~\cite{bowden1999}.

\subsection{The Reconstruction Gap in Forensic Tooling}

The reconstruction options available to forensic practitioners span a spectrum whose endpoints are unsatisfactory in opposite ways:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Classical signature-based carvers} (PhotoRec, Foremost, Scalpel) recover whole files by header-footer matching but perform \emph{no} reconstruction on corrupted or fragmented bytes; corrupted regions remain corrupted on output~\cite{pal2009evolution,garfinkel2007carving}.
  \item \textbf{Classical interpolation and inpainting} (bilinear, PDE diffusion, per-image dictionary learning) reconstruct corrupted regions from the image under examination and therefore avoid external learned priors; they are the strongest baselines in this paper. Their standard implementations do not persist a comparable audit record in the evaluated harness. However, deterministic classical methods could be instrumented to record their operations, so the absence of a released record is not evidence of intrinsic untraceability.
  \item \textbf{Deep-learning inpainters} (generative adversarial network (GAN)-based, diffusion-based) reconstruct with high perceptual fidelity but produce content sampled from a learned prior, which is, in the formal sense, hallucinated~\cite{yu2018generative,suvorov2022resolution,ho2020denoising,rombach2022high,lugmayr2022repaint}.
\end{enumerate}

Classical methods already provide non-deep-learning reconstruction. The unresolved problem is to combine exact preservation of observed pixels, explicit reconstruction assumptions, deterministic execution and a persisted record that supports examination of how selected imputed pixels respond to the observed input. CSFR targets this combination and is positioned against the classical middle, which is the most relevant comparison for this purpose. Its theoretical recovery result applies to an idealised constrained estimator, while its released implementation and empirical evaluation are reported separately.

\subsection{Motivating Evidence from Prior Work}

Bounded reconstruction supports reliable downstream analysis. Fragment classification and carving pipelines degrade when their inputs are corrupted. File-carving studies show that fragmentation and partial overwrites occur in field-recovered media~\cite{garfinkel2007carving,pal2009evolution}. Analyses of AI-assisted forensic tools also identify silent performance failure and miscalibrated confidence on damaged inputs as reliability risks~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations,horsman2024sources,arslan2024reliability}. A reconstruction stage should therefore repair supported regions and identify regions that require review.

\subsection{Research Question}
\label{sec:intro:rq}

The paper addresses one question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{What does downstream forensic analysis gain or lose when a deterministic, constraint-driven procedure reconstructs corrupted decoded-image regions, preserves observed pixels and records approximate local input sensitivity, instead of using an external-prior inpainter that may achieve higher reconstruction quality?}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Constraint-driven reconstruction framework.} We formalise an idealised regularised inverse problem with four constraint families and state the conditions under which that estimator admits a recovery bound. We distinguish this theoretical estimator from the fixed-budget implementation evaluated in the experiments (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).
  \item \textbf{CSFR solver.} We implement a deterministic, training-free decoded-pixel reconstruction procedure comprising input preparation, constraint formulation, optimisation, terminal fragment-consistency projection and an audit stage. For sampled imputed pixels, the audit stage records an approximate local sensitivity of the target objective to observed inputs (Section~\ref{sec:framework}).
  \item \textbf{Evaluation protocol.} We report PSNR, SSIM, constraint-violation measures, a distributional Hallucination Risk Proxy and an implementation-level Audit Record Coverage measure. These study-defined indicators are reported separately from any claim about legal admissibility (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
  \item \textbf{Same-harness evaluation with inference.} A released comparison against six baselines, five classical and one external-prior generative inpainter, with a constraint ablation, paired cluster-robust tests with effect sizes, an iteration-budget analysis, and external validation on a disjoint corpus (Sections~\ref{sec:results}, \ref{sec:results:external}).
  \item \textbf{Downstream utility measurement.} Corrupted digits are reconstructed by each method and classified by a frozen network. Accuracy, confidence frequency, joint confident-error rates and conditional confident-error rates are reported with bootstrap intervals. The results expose an accuracy--coverage trade-off rather than establishing a single downstream winner (Section~\ref{sec:results:downstream}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Compressed sensing establishes that sparse signals are recoverable from few incoherent linear measurements by convex $\ell_{1}$-minimisation~\cite{candes2006robust,donoho2006compressed,candes2006stable}, with the Restricted Isometry Property~\cite{candes2008restricted} giving a sufficient condition for stable recovery. The fit to forensic reconstruction is close: fragments are approximately sparse in DCT or wavelet bases~\cite{mallat2009wavelet}, the corruption operator is linear, and the idealised recovery problem has a closed form that can be inspected. Total-variation~\cite{rudin1992nonlinear} and Tikhonov~\cite{tikhonov1977solutions} regularisation and dictionary-based sparse coding~\cite{elad2006image,mairal2009online,aharon2006ksvd} are mature in non-forensic imaging. What distinguishes this work is restricting the objective to constraints whose violation carries forensic meaning, and rejecting priors external to the fragment under examination.

Classical inpainting fills holes from surrounding image content. Examples include PDE-based diffusion~\cite{bertalmio2000image} and exemplar-based texture synthesis~\cite{criminisi2004region}. This form of controlled imputation is closer to evidential use than a learned prior, although the filled pixels still come from heuristics rather than bounded transformations of the input. Generative inpainting instead samples from a prior fitted to an external corpus. Methods include GAN completion~\cite{yu2018generative}, Fourier-convolutional large-mask inpainting~\cite{suvorov2022resolution}, denoising~\cite{ho2020denoising} and latent~\cite{rombach2022high} diffusion, RePaint~\cite{lugmayr2022repaint}, pluralistic completion~\cite{zheng2019pluralistic}, and latent- and gradient-guided inpainters~\cite{corneanu2024latentpaint,grechka2024gradpaint}. Learned restoration more broadly now spans unfolded proximal and generalised unrolling networks~\cite{le2024unfolded,mou2022deep}, compression-artefact reduction~\cite{ma2024sensitivity} and blind multi-distribution denoising~\cite{zhang2024scalable}; these share with generative inpainting the dependence on weights fitted outside the fragment under examination. The claim about this family is narrow. Generative restoration may be the better tool for triage and lead generation. In evidential use, however, sampling from a learned prior prevents a closed error characterisation and a derivation of each output pixel from the examined input. This concern applies when a reconstruction or a conclusion drawn from it is tendered to a finder of fact (Section~\ref{sec:discussion:admissibility}). LaMa~\cite{suvorov2022resolution} is evaluated on the paper's corpus as an external-prior baseline, so the comparison rests on measured results rather than citation alone.

File carving~\cite{garfinkel2007carving,pal2009evolution,poisel2013advanced,mikus2005evaluation}, including content-based fragment identification~\cite{calhoun2008sceadan} using entropy measures~\cite{shannon1948entropy} and the recovery of orphaned JPEG fragments whose headers are missing~\cite{uzun2015carving}, addresses reassembly, meaning locating and ordering fragments, rather than reconstruction of corrupted bytes. Learned repair of bitstream-corrupted JPEGs~\cite{liu2023bitstream} works at the codec level and introduces a learned prior at that stage. CSFR complements a carver by operating in the decoded pixel domain on the fragments it has already located.

Daubert~\cite{daubert1993} and SWGDE guidance~\cite{swgde2023} emphasise testability, error characterisation and documentation; Farid~\cite{farid2009digital} surveys the legal context. Hallucination is also a structural risk in regularised inversion. Bhadra et al.~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations} show that a data-consistent reconstruction can contain features absent from the object, particularly in the measurement operator's null space, and Tivnan et al.~\cite{tivnan2024hallucination} propose a related image-quality index. The forensics literature has begun to formalise tool error and its disclosure~\cite{horsman2024sources,arslan2024reliability}. CSFR does not remove the null-space problem. It limits the implemented reconstruction through regularisation and global range clipping, preserves observed pixels at release, and records approximate sensitivity for selected imputed pixels. These mechanisms make assumptions and processing choices inspectable, but they do not certify the correctness of missing content.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Observation model and scope}

A grey-level patch $X \in [0,255]^{H \times W}$ with $N = HW$ pixels is observed on a set $\Omega$ of positions, recorded as a binary mask $\mathbf{M}$ with $\mathbf{M}_{rc} = 1$ on $\Omega$. The observed values $Y_{\Omega} = X_{\Omega}$ are known exactly; the remaining pixels, the hole $\Omega^{c}$, are unknown. Observation is noiseless on $\Omega$ by assumption, because an observed pixel is evidence and is reproduced rather than denoised. What happens when $\Omega$ itself is wrong is measured in Section~\ref{sec:results:maskerr}.

\textbf{Domain of reconstruction.} CSFR operates on decoded pixel arrays and does not recover the encoded bitstream. Locating, ordering and decoding fragments is the work of the carver and decoder that precede it, and re-encoding a repaired file is outside the method. The experiments use two kinds of input: synthetic masks applied to decoded images (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), and JPEG files whose bytes were corrupted and then decoded by a standard decoder, with three ways of obtaining the mask (Section~\ref{sec:results:jpeg}). Every statement about the record refers to the pixel-domain map from $(Y_{\Omega}, \mathbf{M})$ to the output.

\subsection{Objective and constraints}
\label{sec:problem:objective}

Let $A = D_{H} X D_{W}^{\top}$ be the orthonormal two-dimensional DCT-II coefficients of $X$, where $D_{n}$ is the $n \times n$ DCT-II matrix, and let
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\varepsilon}(z) = \sqrt{z^{2} + \varepsilon^{2}} - \varepsilon
\end{equation}
be the pseudo-Huber penalty~\cite{charbonnier1997deterministic}, which is smooth, strictly convex, quadratic for $|z| \ll \varepsilon$ and close to $|z| - \varepsilon$ for $|z| \gg \varepsilon$. Forward differences $(\nabla_{v} X)_{rc} = X_{r+1,c} - X_{rc}$ and $(\nabla_{h} X)_{rc} = X_{r,c+1} - X_{rc}$ are taken only between pixels inside the patch (Neumann boundary); there are $N_{v} = (H-1)W$ and $N_{h} = H(W-1)$ of them. The high-frequency index set is $\mathcal{H} = \{(k,l) : k/(H-1) + l/(W-1) \ge \csfrHFcut\}$. CSFR computes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
X^{\star} = \arg\min_{X}\; J(X) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad X_{\Omega} = Y_{\Omega}, \quad 0 \le X \le 255,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:J}
\begin{aligned}
J(X) = {} & \frac{\lambda_{\ell_1}}{N} \sum_{(k,l) \neq (0,0)} \phi_{\varepsilon}(A_{kl})
\;+\; \frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{FC}}}{N} \sum_{(k,l) \in \mathcal{H}} A_{kl}^{2} \\
& +\; \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} \Bigl[ \frac{1}{N_{v}} \sum_{r,c} \phi_{\varepsilon}\bigl((\nabla_{v} X)_{rc}\bigr) + \frac{1}{N_{h}} \sum_{r,c} \phi_{\varepsilon}\bigl((\nabla_{h} X)_{rc}\bigr) \Bigr],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\ell_1} = \csfrLamLone$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} = \csfrLamTV$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{FC}} = \csfrLamFC$ and $\varepsilon = \csfrEps$ grey level for every patch, corpus and corruption family. Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} is the problem the released code solves; there is no separate idealised programme and no penalised surrogate. Four choices in it are deliberate.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Each term is a mean over its index set rather than a sum, so one set of weights applies to every patch size, including the full-resolution images of Section~\ref{sec:results:fullres}.
  \item The DC coefficient is excluded from the sparsity term. It carries the mean intensity, which is not sparse, and penalising it would pull the mean of the hole toward zero.
  \item There is no data-fidelity term. Fragment consistency is a constraint, not a penalty, so the observed pixels are never traded against smoothness.
  \item The weights are those of the released configuration (\texttt{configs/\allowbreak csfr\allowbreak\_sweep\allowbreak\_2d.yaml}). They were fixed before the GovDocs1 and Kodak corpora of this paper were assembled, and no weight was changed for any experiment reported here.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:constraints} maps the four constraint families of the method onto Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective}. C1 and C3 are hard constraints that every iterate satisfies; C2 and C4 are penalty terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:J}, so a reconstruction can have any amount of variation or high-frequency energy at a cost. For that reason constraint-violation rates are reported only within the CSFR ablation (Section~\ref{sec:results:ablation}) and never as a comparison across methods, which would reward CSFR for the terms it optimises.

\label{sec:problem:c3}
C3 is the global dynamic range of an 8-bit image. Bounds derived from the observed neighbourhood of each pixel would be a stronger constraint, but they are not implemented or evaluated in this paper, and C3 should not be read as a local plausibility check.

\begin{proposition}[unique minimiser]
\label{prop:unique}
Let $\Omega \neq \emptyset$ and $\lambda_{\ell_1} > 0$ or $\lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} > 0$. For every patch $X$ and every set $F \subseteq \Omega^{c}$ of hole pixels the Hessian block $\nabla^{2}_{FF} J(X)$ is positive definite, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} has exactly one minimiser.
\end{proposition}
\textit{Proof.} Since $\phi_{\varepsilon}'' > 0$, each term of $J$ has a positive semidefinite Hessian. The DCT is orthonormal, so the Hessian of the sparsity term vanishes only on directions whose DCT is zero outside the DC coefficient, which are the constant patches; the Hessian of the TV term vanishes only on directions with zero differences, which on the connected pixel grid are again the constants. A constant direction supported on $F$ is zero, because it vanishes on $\Omega \neq \emptyset$. Hence $\nabla^{2}_{FF} J \succ 0$ and $J$ is strictly convex on the feasible set, which is non-empty, convex and compact, so the minimiser exists and is unique.~$\square$

The reconstruction is therefore a property of $(Y_{\Omega}, \mathbf{M})$ and the fixed constants. The initialisation, the solver and the iteration count affect only how closely it is approximated, and Section~\ref{sec:framework:solver} bounds that approximation for every patch.

\subsection{What the observed pixels can determine}
\label{sec:problem:oracle}

The formulation is motivated by sparse recovery: natural patches are approximately sparse in the DCT, and convex $\ell_{1}$ minimisation subject to the observations recovers a sparse signal from few incoherent samples~\cite{candes2006robust,donoho2006compressed}. The pixel and DCT bases are highly incoherent (scaled mutual coherence $\sqrt{N}\mu = \oracleCoherenceScaled$ on a scale from $1$ to $\sqrt{N}$~\cite{candes2007sparsity}), but the recovery results built on incoherence assume that the observed positions are drawn uniformly at random. That describes scattered loss and not bursts, overwrites or truncation, and no recovery condition of this kind can be verified for an individual mask.

We therefore replace a recovery bound with a measurement. For each patch, let $S$ be the positions of its $\kappa$ largest-magnitude DCT coefficients, with $\kappa$ equal to 5\% of the pixels, and let $\Phi_{S}$ be the corresponding basis images. The oracle reconstruction fits $\Phi_{S}$ to the observed pixels by least squares. The oracle knows the true support, which no method does. Its hole PSNR describes what a $\kappa$-sparse DCT model can recover from that mask when the support is known, and the condition number of the observed rows of $\Phi_{S}$ measures how well the observations determine the coefficients on that support. Both describe the difficulty of a mask; neither is a bound on any method. Section~\ref{sec:results:oracle} reports them per corruption family.

\section{Solver, Certificate and Sensitivity Record}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Projected accelerated gradient with restart}
\label{sec:framework:solver}

The gradient of $J$ is Lipschitz-continuous with constant
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lipschitz}
L = \frac{\lambda_{\ell_1}}{N\varepsilon} + \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} \Bigl( \frac{4}{N_{v}\varepsilon} + \frac{4}{N_{h}\varepsilon} \Bigr) + \frac{2\lambda_{\mathrm{FC}}}{N},
\end{equation}
because $\phi_{\varepsilon}'' \le 1/\varepsilon$, the DCT is orthonormal and each one-dimensional difference operator has squared norm at most four. The feasible set $\mathcal{C} = \{X : X_{\Omega} = Y_{\Omega},\ 0 \le X \le 255\}$ fixes the observed coordinates and boxes the others. Because $Y_{\Omega}$ lies in the box, its Euclidean projection is exact and acts coordinate by coordinate: clip to $[0,255]$, then reset the observed pixels to $Y_{\Omega}$.

CSFR minimises Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} by projected FISTA~\cite{beck2009fast} with step $1/L$ and the gradient-based adaptive restart of O'Donoghue and Cand\`{e}s~\cite{odonoghue2015adaptive} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:csfr}), in double precision, starting from the zero-filled composite. The projection is applied at every iteration, so every iterate satisfies C1 and C3 exactly. There is no terminal correction step, and the released output is an iterate of the method rather than a post-processed version of one. The gradient and the Hessian--vector products are evaluated in closed form. For images with a side above 128 pixels, the DCT is computed with one FFT per axis~\cite{makhoul1980fast}.

\textbf{Stopping rule and certificate.} Every \csfrCheckEvery\ iterations the solver evaluates the Frank--Wolfe gap~\cite{frank1956algorithm}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gap}
G(X) = \max_{Z \in \mathcal{C}} \langle \nabla J(X),\, X - Z \rangle = \sum_{i \in \Omega^{c}} g_{i}\,(X_{i} - z_{i}),
\end{equation}
where $g = \nabla J(X)$, $z_{i} = 255$ where $g_{i} < 0$ and $z_{i} = 0$ elsewhere. Convexity gives $J(X) - J(X^{\star}) \le \langle \nabla J(X), X - X^{\star} \rangle \le G(X)$, so $G$ bounds the suboptimality of the current iterate in objective units without knowledge of $X^{\star}$. A patch stops when $G \le \csfrTol$ and is then frozen. A patch that reaches \csfrMaxIter\ iterations is reported as not converged; it is never silently accepted. Each patch's certificate is written beside its output and copied into every record manifest. It contains the iterations, the converged flag, $G$, $G/J$, the final projected-step size and the objective, together with $L$, the step and the full configuration.

The tolerance was calibrated, not chosen. On \calibNcells\ cells of the DFRWS corpus (\calibCells; \calibNpatches\ patches each), every patch was first solved to a reference gap of $\recRefTol$, close to the double-precision floor, and then at each tolerance from $\calibGridLoose$ to $\calibGridTight$. The criterion compares each solve with the minimiser, not with the ground truth, so it fixes numerical accuracy and cannot tune reconstruction quality. The default is the loosest tolerance whose 99th-percentile per-patch maximum pixel error against the reference is below $0.01$ grey levels in every cell. That tolerance is $\csfrTol$: in the worst cell the 99th-percentile error is $\calibPctlErrMax$ grey levels and the mean hole PSNR is within $\calibPsnrDiffMax$\,dB of the reference. The next looser value, $\calibNextTol$, fails on \calibNextFailCells. No calibration solve reached the iteration cap, and at the default tolerance the median iteration count ranged from \calibItersMin\ to \calibItersMax\ across the cells.

Two independent checks agree with the reference solves. L-BFGS-B (SciPy) on the same objective and box reached them within $\calibLbfgsbMaxDiff$ grey levels on the first \calibLbfgsbN\ patches of each cell. Zero-filled and Navier--Stokes initialisations, each solved to $\calibInitTol$, agree within $\calibInitMaxDiff$ grey levels, as Proposition~\ref{prop:unique} requires.

Two simpler stopping rules were rejected on measurement. A relative gap $G/J$ cannot be met on flat patches, where the double-precision floor of $G$ is a large fraction of a small $J$. A projected-step rule, which stops when no pixel moves by more than a threshold, stops far from the optimum in large holes: there the gradient is close to zero until information has propagated in from the boundary. On CF3~L5, a threshold of $\calibRejStepThresh$ grey levels stopped at a mean hole PSNR of \calibRejStepPsnr\,dB, against \calibRejRefPsnr\,dB at the minimiser.

\textbf{Fixed-budget comparison arm.} The configuration of the previously released implementation (\texttt{src/\allowbreak csfr\allowbreak\_reconstruct\allowbreak\_2d.py}) is evaluated only as a comparison arm (Section~\ref{sec:results:solver}). It runs Adam with learning rate $2.0$~\cite{kingma2015adam} on a penalised form of the objective with a quadratic data term, for a budget of $600$ iterations, with C3 applied by clipping after each step and C1 once at the end. It carries no certificate. Runtime and memory of both solvers are reported in Section~\ref{sec:results:cost}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{CSFR solver, as implemented in \texttt{src/\allowbreak csfr\allowbreak\_solver.py} (function \texttt{solve}). The patches of a batch are solved independently, and a converged patch is frozen.}
\label{alg:csfr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require observed values $Y$; mask $\mathbf{M}$ ($\mathbf{M}=1$ on $\Omega$); $\lambda_{\ell_1}, \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{FC}}, \varepsilon$; tolerance $\tau$; cap $K$; check interval $c$
\Ensure $\hat{X} \in \mathcal{C}$ and its certificate
\State $L \gets$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:lipschitz}
\State $P(X) \gets \mathbf{M}\odot Y + (1-\mathbf{M})\odot\mathrm{clip}(X, 0, 255)$ \Comment{projection onto $\mathcal{C}$}
\State $X_{0} \gets P(\mathbf{M}\odot Y)$; \; $Z \gets X_{0}$; \; $t \gets 1$
\For{$k = 1, \ldots, K$}
  \State $X_{k} \gets P\bigl(Z - \nabla J(Z)/L\bigr)$
  \If{$\langle Z - X_{k},\, X_{k} - X_{k-1} \rangle > 0$}
    \State $t \gets 1$ \Comment{adaptive restart}
  \EndIf
  \State $t' \gets \bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 + 4t^{2}}\bigr)/2$
  \State $Z \gets X_{k} + \tfrac{t-1}{t'}\,(X_{k} - X_{k-1})$; \; $t \gets t'$
  \If{$k \bmod c = 0$ \textbf{or} $k = K$}
    \If{$G(X_{k}) \le \tau$} \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap}}
      \State \Return $X_{k}$ with a certificate marked converged
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $X_{K}$ with a certificate marked not converged
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Sensitivity record}
\label{sec:framework:audit}

At the minimiser, let $F = \{ i \in \Omega^{c} : \recBoundMargin < X^{\star}_{i} < 255 - \recBoundMargin \}$ be the free set of hole pixels not at a bound, and $B = \Omega^{c} \setminus F$. Stationarity $\nabla_{F} J(X^{\star}) = 0$ holds on $F$, and $Y_{\Omega}$ enters the problem only through $X_{\Omega} = Y_{\Omega}$. Where strict complementarity holds at the pixels of $B$, so that small perturbations do not change which pixels are at a bound, the implicit function theorem~\cite{dontchev2014implicit} gives the Jacobian of the solution map:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:record}
\frac{\partial X^{\star}_{F}}{\partial Y_{\Omega}} = -\,H_{FF}^{-1} H_{F\Omega}, \qquad H = \nabla^{2} J(X^{\star}).
\end{equation}
$H_{FF}$ is invertible by Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}. The derivative of a pixel in $B$ is zero, and an observed pixel has the identity row because C1 fixes it.

The record of an imputed pixel $i$ is row $i$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:record}. It is computed by solving $H_{FF}\, w = e_{i}$ with conjugate gradients~\cite{hestenes1952methods} and the closed-form Hessian--vector products, and then forming $-H_{\Omega F}\, w$. Columns, the response of every free pixel to one observed pixel, are computed in the same way; they are the transposed view of the same matrix, which a test checks. All record computations use double precision, a relative CG residual of $\recCGrtol$ with a cap of \recCGmax\ iterations, and no damping. Weights smaller than $\recSupport$ times the largest weight are not counted in the reported support. Units are grey levels of the reconstructed pixel per grey level of the observed pixel.

\begin{proposition}[affine record]
\label{prop:affine}
If no hole pixel is at a bound ($B = \emptyset$), every row of Eq.~\eqref{eq:record} sums to one.
\end{proposition}
\textit{Proof.} The sparsity term excludes the DC coefficient, $\mathcal{H}$ does not contain it, and TV acts on differences, so $J(X + c\mathbf{1}) = J(X)$ for every constant $c$ and $H\mathbf{1} = 0$. With $F = \Omega^{c}$, the rows of $H$ indexed by $F$ give $H_{FF}\mathbf{1}_{F} + H_{F\Omega}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega} = 0$, hence $-H_{FF}^{-1} H_{F\Omega}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega} = \mathbf{1}_{F}$.~$\square$

The record is therefore an affine combination of the observed pixels: shifting every observed value by $c$ shifts every free pixel by $c$. The barycentric weights of linear interpolation also sum to one, but the CSFR weights are dense and can be negative, so they must not be read as contribution shares. The proposition also gives every record row a free accuracy check. Its affine defect, $1 - \sum_{k} w_{k}$, is zero for the exact Jacobian, and every manifest reports it beside the CG residual. For the same reason the linear system is not damped. A damped system $(H_{FF} + \gamma I)\,w = e_{i}$ is a common safeguard for CG, but it is unnecessary here because $H_{FF}$ is positive definite, and for any $\gamma > 0$ it violates Proposition~\ref{prop:affine}.

\textbf{What the record is and is not.} The record is the derivative of the map from observations to reconstruction, evaluated at the released output. It states how the reconstructed value would change, to first order, if an observed pixel had a different value. It is not a decomposition of the pixel value, it does not say which observations caused the content, and it says nothing about whether the reconstructed value is close to the truth. A first-order statement can also fail for perturbations of practical size, which is why the record is validated.

\textbf{Validation by perturb-and-re-solve.} For a queried observed pixel $k$ and a perturbation $\delta$, the predicted column $c_{\mathrm{pred}} = \partial X^{\star}_{F} / \partial Y_{k}$ is compared with the measured response $c_{\mathrm{act}}(\delta) = \bigl(X^{\star}(Y + \delta e_{k}) - X^{\star}(Y)\bigr)_{F} / \delta$, obtained by solving the perturbed problem again. The sign of $\delta$ is reversed where $Y_{k} + \delta$ would leave the range. The relative error is $e(\delta) = \|c_{\mathrm{act}} - c_{\mathrm{pred}}\|_{2} / \|c_{\mathrm{act}}\|_{2}$ over $F$, and it is measured twice:
\begin{itemize}
  \item against re-solves at a gap of $\recRefTol$, which asks whether the record is the derivative of the solution map;
  \item against re-solves at the operational tolerance $\csfrTol$, which asks what an examiner who re-runs the tool would observe.
\end{itemize}
The \emph{Sensitivity Record Fidelity} (SRF) of a set of queries is the fraction with $e(1) \le 0.10$. It is reported for both references, together with the sign agreement on the \recTopK\ largest predicted entries. Column queries are observed pixels at Chebyshev distance 1, 3 and at least 8 from the hole. Row queries, which the manifests display, are imputed pixels at distance 1, 3 and at the deepest point of the hole (Section~\ref{sec:results:fidelity}). Each manifest records the observation hashes, the objective and solver configuration, the certificate and the record parameters. For each query it records the largest weights, the support, the CG iterations and residual, the affine defect and the time taken. \texttt{src/\allowbreak sensitivity.py} implements the record.

\textbf{Comparison records.} A record is not unique to CSFR, because classical interpolators are deterministic and some have exact records. Linear interpolation on the Delaunay triangulation of the observed pixels has an exact, sparse Jacobian: each imputed pixel is a barycentric combination of at most three observed pixels. Telea and Navier--Stokes inpainting are deterministic but have no closed-form Jacobian, so their sensitivity can be obtained only by finite differences, at the cost of one re-run per observed pixel. Section~\ref{sec:results:records} compares these records with that of CSFR in type, exactness, support, fidelity and cost. What the convex formulation adds is a record for a global, non-local reconstruction, together with a certificate that the released output is the minimiser at which the record is evaluated.

\subsection{Warnings}
\label{sec:framework:warnings}

The implementation issues two warnings and states one limitation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A patch whose solve reaches the iteration cap is released with a not-converged flag, and its certificate says so.
  \item The Bounded-Unknown diagnostic is the isolated-missing fraction of the mask, the share of hole pixels with no observed four-neighbour. It is computed from the mask alone (\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak run\allowbreak\_downstream.py}), and a patch where it exceeds one half is labelled Bounded-Unknown for review. The label does not withhold the numerical output. Whether the statistic predicts reconstruction failure well enough to act on is tested against a rule fixed before the data were seen (Section~\ref{sec:results:bu}).
  \item The method does not certify that reconstructed content is correct. The certificate concerns the optimisation and the record concerns the solution map, so both should be treated as aids to examination, not as proof of recoverability.
\end{enumerate}
No stage uses a learned prior trained on data from outside the patch under examination.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets and Fragmentation Types}

We distinguish the data on which CSFR results are executed from the data that motivate and contextualise them:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{DFRWS 2006 image patches (executed CSFR evaluation).} All quantitative CSFR results (Section~\ref{sec:results}) use $N = 300$ grayscale $64{\times}64$ patches carved from the DFRWS 2006 forensic challenge disk image, corrupted synthetically across four families (CF1 random pixel loss, CF2 burst block corruption, CF3 half-image overwrite, CF4 truncation) at five severity levels. Synthetic corruption on intact patches yields the per-pixel ground truth required for PSNR and SSIM.
  \item \textbf{GovDocs1 patches (external validation).} An independent corpus of $N = 300$ grayscale $64{\times}64$ patches from 176 distinct JPEGs in the GovDocs1 public document corpus~\cite{garfinkel2009bringing}, separate from DFRWS 2006. We applied the identical protocol with no parameter re-tuning (Section~\ref{sec:results:external}).
  \item \textbf{Nick Mikus carving images (naturally corrupted, full-resolution, colour).} The DFTT FAT32 and Ext2 carving test images~\cite{mikus2005evaluation}, containing real carved colour JPEGs including one with deliberately damaged header. These provide full-resolution colour case studies (Section~\ref{sec:results:external}).
  \item \textbf{Protocol extensions (specified, not executed).} The FFT-75 SC5 (DS-03) and SC6 (DS-04) JPEG partitions define the byte-domain scaling path. They are specified here for replication and remain future evaluation targets.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Patch sampling protocol.} Both patch corpora are built by \texttt{scripts/extract\allowbreak\_patches.py} under one seeded protocol, stated here in full because the review of an earlier draft correctly identified the sampling as under-specified. JPEG byte ranges are signature-carved from the source, decoded, and kept if the shorter side is at least 96 pixels. Each kept image is diced on a \emph{non-overlapping} grid with stride equal to the patch size, so no two patches in a corpus share a pixel and there is no leakage between them. Patches whose intensity standard deviation is below 10 levels are discarded, because PSNR is degenerate on flat content and such patches would otherwise dominate the aggregate. The retained patches are sampled without replacement to the target count under a fixed seed. Every patch's source image, its coordinates within that image, and the SHA-256 of the carved byte range are recorded in the released manifest.

\paragraph{Clustering and the statistical unit.} The two corpora differ sharply in concentration. The 300 DFRWS 2006 patches come from six source images, the two largest contributing $77\,\%$ between them; the 300 GovDocs1 patches come from 176 images, none contributing more than $1.7\,\%$. Patches from one image share content, capture conditions and quantisation, so treating $N=300$ as 300 independent units overstates precision on the DFRWS corpus. Section~\ref{sec:results:stats} therefore reports patch-level and cluster-robust results side by side, and treats GovDocs1 as the better-designed sample. The DFRWS corpus is reported as constituted so the numbers remain those of the released artefacts.

The four corruption families abstract corruption mechanisms documented in the file-carving literature~\cite{garfinkel2007carving,pal2009evolution} into the pixel domain: scattered loss (CF1), localised burst damage (CF2), and the two half-image erasures (CF3/CF4) that model contiguous overwrite and truncation of the decoded patch.






\subsection{Baselines}

Six baselines are evaluated under the identical harness: same masks, same seeds, same metrics. Five are classical methods whose imputed values are derived from the image under examination. The sixth, LaMa, is included as an external-prior baseline so that reconstruction quality can be compared empirically with the audit-oriented CSFR implementation on this paper's own corpus rather than argued from citation (Section~\ref{sec:related}). The study does not use this comparison to determine legal admissibility.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Zero-fill.} Sets $\hat{x}_{i} = 0$ for $i \in \Omega^{c}$. This trivial lower bound establishes baseline performance.
  \item \textbf{Linear interpolation.} Bilinear interpolation from $\Omega \cap \mathcal{N}(i)$.
  \item \textbf{Telea inpainting.} The fast-marching method~\cite{telea2004image}, via OpenCV (\texttt{cv2.inpaint}, radius 3).
  \item \textbf{Navier--Stokes inpainting.} The PDE formulation of Bertalmio et al.~\cite{bertalmio2000image}, via the same OpenCV call (radius 3).
  \item \textbf{Dictionary-learning sparse coding.} Online dictionary learning~\cite{mairal2009online,aharon2006ksvd} fitted to each corrupted image: a 64-atom dictionary of $8{\times}8$ tiles learned from fully observed tiles, with each incomplete tile reconstructed by orthogonal matching pursuit ($8$-sparse) on its observed pixels only; observed pixels are retained exactly. This baseline resembles CSFR's forensic posture: it draws imputations only from the fragment's own content. Tiles with no observed pixels fall back to the observed-region mean.
  \item \textbf{LaMa external-prior inpainting}~\cite{suvorov2022resolution}. The large-mask Fourier-convolutional inpainter, run off the shelf from the released \texttt{big-lama} checkpoint distributed with the \texttt{simple-lama-inpainting} package, with no fine-tuning on any corpus used here. Masks, seeds and metrics are identical to the classical methods. Three configuration details matter to anyone replicating it. Inference runs at the patch's native resolution, since the network is fully convolutional and $64$ and $32$ are both divisible by $8$; an earlier configuration that upsampled to $256$ and back cost $8$ to $22$\,dB and was discarded on measurement. The grayscale patch is replicated across three channels for input and the output is taken back to one channel. The model output is composited onto the observed region, so observed pixels are returned bit-exact and the only thing this baseline contributes is what it imputes, which is what makes it comparable to the five classical methods and to CSFR. A single forward pass with no sampling makes it deterministic on fixed hardware.
\end{enumerate}

Two scope qualifications on the external-prior baseline are stated where its results are reported. It received no privileged run: the primary sweep executed as a single job in which every method reconstructed the same patches under the same masks, with the two PyTorch methods, CSFR and LaMa, on the GPU and the OpenCV and scikit-learn baselines on CPU because those libraries provide no GPU path (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Device therefore affects runtime, which Section~\ref{sec:results:cost} measures separately on a uniform CPU path for every method, and not the reconstructions the quality tables compare. The run persisted cell-level metrics without the per-patch reconstructions the paired tests consume, so LaMa appears in Tables~\ref{tab:psnr-ssim}, \ref{tab:cvr}, \ref{tab:hrp} and~\ref{tab:downstream} as cell means, is absent from the paired analysis of Section~\ref{sec:results:stats}, and was not run on the external corpus of Section~\ref{sec:results:external}.

Beyond LaMa, the wider generative family, GAN completion~\cite{yu2018generative}, denoising~\cite{ho2020denoising} and latent~\cite{rombach2022high} diffusion, and diffusion inpainting variants~\cite{lugmayr2022repaint,zheng2019pluralistic}, is discussed from the literature only and is not run under this harness. These methods are therefore excluded from the implementation-level Audit Record Coverage comparison of Section~\ref{sec:results:ts}. One measured representative keeps the comparison empirical while the family-level claim remains citation-based.

\subsection{Forensic-Appropriate Metrics}

Five metrics are computed per reconstruction:

\paragraph{M1, M2: PSNR and SSIM.} Peak signal-to-noise ratio against ground truth over the whole patch, capped at $100$\,dB for numerically exact reconstructions, and structural similarity as defined by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2004image} on the full reconstructed image. Every evaluated method returns observed pixels unaltered, so the preserved region contributes near-zero error uniformly and inflates absolute values without favouring any method. That convention is kept because it matches the released harness, and Section~\ref{sec:results:stats} reports the stricter imputation-only variant, taking the mean over $\Omega^{c}$ alone, to confirm the between-method conclusions survive scoring only the pixels that were reconstructed.

\paragraph{M3: Constraint Violation Rate (CVR).} The fraction of pixels violating a given constraint family,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CVR}_{c} \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_{c}} \mathbb{I}\bigl(\, \hat{x}_{i} \text{ violates } c \,\bigr),
\end{equation}
reported per family rather than pooled, since the four families carry different forensic weight. The constraint definitions of Section~\ref{sec:problem} are stated at the level of the feasibility set; scoring them requires committing to operational thresholds, which we state here in full because they determine every number in Table~\ref{tab:cvr}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1} counts observed pixels whose reconstructed value moves by more than $0.5$ intensity levels, $\{i \in \Omega : |\hat{x}_{i} - y_{i}| > 0.5\}$; the half-level tolerance absorbs float round-trip error and nothing else.
  \item \textbf{C2} counts adjacent-pixel differences exceeding a fixed threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{TV}} = 64$ intensity levels, averaged over the horizontal and vertical difference fields. This is a global constant, not the position-dependent $\tau_{\mathrm{TV}}(i)$ of Section~\ref{sec:problem}: a per-pixel bound estimated from each pixel's own observed neighbourhood would make the metric a function of the mask, and therefore not comparable across corruption families. The constant threshold is the weaker but comparable choice.
  \item \textbf{C3} counts pixels outside the implemented global range $[0,255]$ of Section~\ref{sec:problem:c3}. This is also the bound enforced by the solver. Because all evaluated methods return values in this range, C3 is mainly informative in the CSFR ablation of Section~\ref{sec:results:ablation}, which removes clipping; it does not measure neighbourhood plausibility.
  \item \textbf{C4} counts high-frequency DCT coefficients whose magnitude exceeds $32$, where the high-frequency set $\mathcal{H}$ is the coefficients with normalised index sum $\ge 0.5$. This is a per-coefficient magnitude test rather than the energy-ratio form of Section~\ref{sec:problem}; the two agree in direction but the per-coefficient form is what the released harness computes.
\end{itemize}
Thresholds are fixed in \texttt{configs/\allowbreak csfr\allowbreak\_sweep\allowbreak\_2d.yaml} and identical for all methods. For CSFR, C1 and C3 are zero by construction on released outputs (terminal projection and per-step clipping respectively); C2 and C4 are penalised softly and reported as measured. Reading these columns as evidence about \emph{arbitrary} constraint sets would over-claim: they are measurements against these stated thresholds.

\paragraph{M4: Hallucination Risk Proxy (HRP).} The mean standardised deviation of imputed pixels from the intensity distribution of the observed fragment region: with $\mu_{\Omega}$ and $\sigma_{\Omega}$ the mean and standard deviation of the observed pixels $y_{\Omega}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HRP} \;=\; \frac{1}{|\Omega^{c}|}\sum_{i \in \Omega^{c}} \frac{\bigl|\hat{x}_{i} - \mu_{\Omega}\bigr|}{\max(\sigma_{\Omega}, 1)}
\end{equation}
HRP is expressed in observed-region standard deviations, so a value near or below $1$ indicates imputations statistically consistent with the fragment's own content. HRP measures distributional departure from the observed-region intensity distribution. It can flag out-of-distribution imputations but cannot identify incorrect values that remain within that distribution. The global C3 bound does not guarantee a low HRP. HRP is therefore reported as a descriptive proxy, not as evidence that a reconstruction is free from hallucinated structure. This definition is the one implemented in \texttt{src/csfr\allowbreak\_reconstruct\allowbreak\_2d.py} and used in all reported tables.

\paragraph{M5: Audit Record Coverage (ARC).} The fraction of declared sensitivity queries for which the released software produced a usable audit artefact:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ARC} \;=\; \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{i \in Q} \mathbb{I}\bigl(\, s_{i} \text{ is finite and non-empty} \,\bigr)
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the declared set of queried imputed pixels and $s_{i}$ is the approximate local sensitivity vector returned for query $i$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:provenance} (distinct from the sparse coefficient vector $\alpha$). ARC is an implementation-level completeness indicator. It records whether the released software produced a usable audit artefact for the declared queries. It does not establish that the sensitivity vector is exact, causally complete or unique, and it does not assign an intrinsic score to methods that were not instrumented to emit comparable records; for those methods ARC is reported as not available rather than as zero. For CSFR, ARC is measured rather than asserted: for a sample of 16 imputed pixels per probed patch the solve of Eq.~\eqref{eq:provenance} is run, and a query counts as covered when the solve returns a non-empty set of non-negligible weights with a finite residual (Section~\ref{sec:results:ts}). One caveat travels with the score: Eq.~\eqref{eq:provenance} holds at a stationary point, and Section~\ref{sec:results:budget} shows the fixed-budget solver is not at one on the harder masks, so on CF2 and severe-mask cells these weights describe the solver's target rather than a certified property of the returned iterate, and the released manifest labels them accordingly.

\subsection{Reproducibility Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

All experiments are seeded with $\mathrm{seed} \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ for $n = 3$ independent runs per condition; reported values are means over seeds, with per-patch standard deviations ($N = 300$ patches per cell) and between-seed standard deviations recorded in the released summary files. The solver uses the 2-D DCT sparsifying basis with fixed regularisation weights $\lambda_{\ell_1} = 0.02$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} = 0.10$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{FC}} = 0.005$, Adam with learning rate $2.0$, and a fixed budget of $600$ iterations with no early stopping; the C3 bounds are enforced by projection (clipping to $[0, 255]$) after every step. All hyper-parameters are stated in \texttt{configs/\allowbreak csfr\allowbreak\_sweep\allowbreak\_2d.yaml}. The released results come from two environments, and which produced which is recorded in the artefacts rather than asserted here. The primary sweep of Section~\ref{sec:results} and the downstream experiment of Section~\ref{sec:results:downstream} were executed as single jobs on Linux under Python 3.12.13 with PyTorch 2.10.0+cu128 on an NVIDIA T4, with \texttt{device=cuda} recorded in all 60 per-cell metric files of the primary sweep. The external validation of Section~\ref{sec:results:external} and the runtime benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:results:cost} were executed on Windows 11 (x64) on CPU under Python 3.14.6 with PyTorch 2.13.0+cpu, which is the environment pinned in \texttt{requirements.txt}. The harness records the interpreter, platform, and exact version of every dependency into the \texttt{run\allowbreak\_metadata.json} of each sweep directory, together with the SHA-256 of the patch array consumed; those files, not this paragraph, are the authoritative record, and the committed corpora verify against the hashes the runs recorded. Two released artefacts predate the capture and carry no environment record: the iteration-budget sweep behind Table~S3 and the per-method downstream runs other than the external prior. The reference implementation is released alongside \texttt{src/csfr\allowbreak\_reconstruct\allowbreak\_2d.py} and \texttt{scripts/run\allowbreak\_csfr\allowbreak\_sweep.py}.

\paragraph{Determinism.} The solver contains no stochastic element: initialisation is the zero-fill composite, Adam is deterministic given the gradient sequence, and no dropout, sampling, or data-order shuffling occurs. The seed governs only mask generation and the dictionary-learning baseline's fit. Re-running a cell on the same machine and software stack is expected to reproduce $\hat{x}$ bit-for-bit, and the released checkpoints allow this to be checked directly. Determinism \emph{across} hardware and library versions is a stronger property that we do not claim: floating-point reduction order differs between BLAS builds and accelerators, so a reconstruction produced on different hardware may differ in the last bits. For evidential use this means the audit artefact to preserve is the reconstruction together with its \texttt{run\allowbreak\_metadata.json}, not the expectation that any machine will reproduce it exactly.

\subsection{Reconstruction Quality vs. Corruption Severity}
\label{sec:results:psnr}

Table~\ref{tab:psnr-ssim} reports quality for CSFR and the six same-harness baselines at severity levels L1/L3/L5 (full grid released; means over seeds $\{0,1,2\}$, $N{=}300$ patches per cell). CSFR attains the best quality on random pixel loss (CF1) at every severity, and that lead survives cluster-robust inference. Elsewhere it does not lead. On the half-image masks it is indistinguishable from the PDE inpainters at L1 and trails them by $7.4$ to $8.4$\,dB from L3 upward, and the external prior LaMa takes the highest PSNR in most cells from L2 up. On burst corruption (CF2) LaMa leads at every severity, by $1.8$\,dB at L1 widening to $7.0$\,dB at L5.

\subsection{Paired Inference}
\label{sec:results:stats}

Table~\ref{tab:psnr-ssim} reports point estimates. This section reports whether the differences survive inference. The analysis plan is fixed and released in \texttt{results/\allowbreak paired\allowbreak\_stats/\allowbreak analysis\allowbreak\_plan.json}; it was specified before the tests were run and is stated here in full.

\paragraph{Analysis plan.} The unit is the image patch, matched across methods: every method reconstructs the same patch under the same mask, so comparisons are paired. Each patch's metric is first averaged over the three seeds, giving one paired observation per patch per cell and avoiding the pseudo-replication that would arise from treating the same 300 patches under three seeds as 900 independent observations. The primary test is the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}, chosen over the paired $t$ because per-patch PSNR is right-skewed and SSIM is bounded; the paired $t$ is reported alongside in the released CSV and agrees throughout. Multiplicity is controlled by the Holm--Bonferroni procedure~\cite{holm1979simple} across all 100 comparisons within each metric (20 cells $\times$ 5 baselines). Effect size is reported as Cohen's $d_{z}$~\cite{cohen1988statistical} and Cliff's $\delta$~\cite{cliff1993dominance}. Intervals are 95\,\% percentile bootstrap with $10^{4}$ resamples. Table~\ref{tab:paired} reports CSFR against the \emph{strongest} baseline in each cell, which is the comparison that can hurt the paper; the full $6 \times 20$ grid is released.

The external-prior baseline is absent from this paired analysis. It was computed in a separate GPU run that persisted cell-level metrics without the per-patch reconstructions the paired tests consume, so its comparison against CSFR rests on the cell means of Table~\ref{tab:psnr-ssim} and is not accompanied by a paired test or an interval. Its margins over CSFR on burst and half-image corruption are large, several decibels in most affected cells, but they are reported here as point estimates and should be read as such. Paired inference against that baseline requires re-running it with per-patch outputs retained, which is left to future work.

\paragraph{Cluster-robust reporting.} The 300 DFRWS patches come from six source images and are not independent draws. Two additional analyses respect this clustering. A cluster bootstrap resamples whole source images rather than patches; this interval is the primary robustness evidence and is roughly three times wider than the patch-level interval. A Wilcoxon test on six per-image mean differences provides a second check. The cluster-level test is uninformative here: with six clusters, the smallest attainable $p$-value is $0.031$, so after correction across 100 comparisons no cell reaches significance. This is a property of the sampling design, not the method, motivating the external corpus (Section~\ref{sec:results:external}).

\paragraph{Findings.} Three conclusions hold; one claim from earlier drafts does not survive.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Under random pixel loss (CF1), CSFR's advantage is consistent and statistically supported.} It beats the strongest baseline (Navier--Stokes) by $1.00$--$1.44$\,dB at every severity. Both the patch-level and the cluster-robust intervals exclude zero at all five levels, and $d_{z}$ ranges from $0.62$ to $1.19$. Cliff's $\delta$ is small ($0.10$--$0.16$) because the per-patch distributions overlap heavily; the effect is a consistent shift rather than a separation of populations, which is what the paired statistics capture and the unpaired ones do not.
  \item \textbf{Under burst corruption (CF2) from L3 upward and structured erasure (CF3/CF4) from L2 upward, CSFR loses, and the deficit is large.} Against the best baseline it gives up $1.7$--$5.2$\,dB on CF2 (L3--L5) and $5.1$--$8.4$\,dB on CF3/CF4, with cluster-robust intervals excluding zero and $|d_{z}|$ up to $1.17$. At the two lowest burst severities (CF2~L1, L2) the loss is not cluster-robust (the six-image cluster interval spans zero), so we report those as indistinguishable rather than as losses. These are not marginal losses where they are robust, and we do not present them as such.
  \item \textbf{The conclusions are essentially unchanged under the stricter, imputation-only PSNR.} Recomputing all 100 comparisons with the mean squared error taken over $\Omega^{c}$ alone rather than the whole patch changes no significance verdict and shifts no estimate by more than $0.13$\,dB. Exactly one of the 100 comparisons changes sign: CSFR versus Telea at CF4~L1 moves from $+0.11$\,dB to $-0.03$\,dB, a reversal of a difference far too small to support a directional claim under either definition. The whole-patch scoring convention is therefore not carrying the comparisons.
  \item \textbf{The claim that CSFR leads on low-severity structured masks is withdrawn.} At CF3~L1 the point estimate favours CSFR by $0.26$\,dB and the patch-level test is significant after correction ($p = 1.3 \times 10^{-3}$), but the cluster-robust interval is $[-0.49, +0.48]$: once the six-image dependence is respected, the sign is not established. At CF4~L1 the estimate is $-0.20$\,dB and is not significant on either analysis. The honest statement is that CSFR and the PDE inpainters are \emph{indistinguishable} on low-severity half-image masks, not that CSFR leads. Earlier drafts of this paper claimed the latter on the strength of the patch-level analysis alone; that claim was an artefact of treating clustered patches as independent.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Constraint Ablation}
\label{sec:results:ablation}

Table~S1 isolates the implemented effects of C2 total variation, C3 global intensity clipping and C4 frequency regularisation. The data-fidelity term (C1) and the $\ell_1$ sparsity prior are the reconstruction engine and are active in every variant. C2 provides the largest average quality gain ($+1.15$\,dB over the C1-only core and the largest SSIM gain). C3 eliminates values outside $[0,255]$ and modestly improves average PSNR ($25.09$ against $24.41$\,dB for the C1-only core), but it does not enforce neighbourhood-derived bounds. C4 reduces the operational high-frequency violation measure and contributes little in isolation. The full configuration gives the highest average PSNR and SSIM among the tested variants together with zero C3 violation; the C1+C4 variant achieves a marginally lower C4 violation rate (1.69\,\% against 1.85\,\%) but drops the clipping that keeps released intensities in range. These results support the implemented penalties; they do not establish local plausibility or per-instance recovery guarantees.




\subsection{Runtime and Memory}
\label{sec:results:cost}

Table~S2 reports wall-clock time and Python-tracked peak allocation per patch, measured on the same CPU path for every method (five repeats of a 32-patch batch; environment recorded in \texttt{results/\allowbreak bench/\allowbreak environment.json}). CSFR's 600-iteration solve is roughly one to two orders of magnitude slower than the PDE and dictionary baselines, tens of milliseconds per $64\times64$ patch against roughly one, consistent with the $O(T \cdot N \log N)$ cost of Section~\ref{sec:limits}. Bilinear interpolation is slow in this implementation because the SciPy Delaunay interpolation it uses scales poorly on scattered masks; it is not a floor on interpolation cost. Peak allocation was measured with \texttt{tracemalloc}, which does not capture all native allocations made by PyTorch, NumPy or OpenCV; the memory values should therefore be read as Python-level allocation indicators rather than complete process-memory measurements. Within that limit, allocation is small and comparable across methods, so CSFR's cost is compute rather than memory. These figures place CSFR in offline analysis rather than real-time triage, and they are the practical price of the fixed, auditable iteration budget.



\subsection{Audit Record Coverage}
\label{sec:results:ts}

CSFR produced a finite, non-empty approximate sensitivity vector for all 16 sampled queries in CF1~L3 and all 16 sampled queries in CF3~L5, giving Audit Record Coverage of $1.00$ for those probes, with mean support of $2867$ and $1216$ observed pixels respectively (the latter smaller because fewer pixels are observed). The comparison implementations did not emit equivalent persisted records in this study and are therefore marked ``not available'' rather than assigned an intrinsic score of zero. Linear interpolation, PDE inpainting and dictionary learning are deterministic and could be instrumented in future work. The present result establishes availability of the CSFR audit artefact, not exclusive traceability of the underlying method.



\subsection{Exploratory quality and auditability comparison}
\label{sec:results:frontier}

Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} plots mean PSNR against a study-defined auditability composite (AC) formed from CVR, normalised HRP and implementation-level Audit Record Coverage. The composite is exploratory. It is not a validated forensic-quality scale and does not measure legal admissibility:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AC} \;=\; w_{1}\,(1 - \mathrm{CVR}) \;+\; w_{2}\,(1 - \mathrm{HRP}_{\mathrm{norm}}) \;+\; w_{3}\,\mathrm{ARC}
\end{equation}
with $w_{1} = w_{2} = w_{3} = 1/3$, and with ARC entered as $0$ for the methods whose evaluated implementations emitted no record, since the composite needs a number where Section~\ref{sec:results:ts} reports ``not available''. With equal weights, CSFR ranks highly because it emits the audit record that the comparison implementations were not configured to produce. This result describes the evaluated software artefacts, not an inherent inability of classical algorithms to support attribution or logging. The component measures in Tables~\ref{tab:cvr} and~\ref{tab:hrp} remain the primary evidence and should be interpreted separately.

\subsection{Iteration-Budget Sensitivity}
\label{sec:results:budget}

The objective is still descending at the end of the 600-iteration budget under burst corruption, the family where CSFR is beaten by every baseline, which raises the question of whether the deficit is premature stopping. Re-solving five cells at budgets of 600, 1200, 2400 and 4800 iterations with everything else fixed (Table~S3, a 100-patch seed-0 subset) indicates that it largely is. On CF1 the objective has converged by 600 and further iterations move PSNR by hundredths of a decibel. On CF2 and the severe half-image masks it has not: at CF2~L5 CSFR trails bilinear by $4.6$\,dB at 600 iterations and leads it by $1.2$\,dB at 2400, at CF2~L3 a $0.8$\,dB deficit becomes a $1.5$\,dB lead, and the CF3/CF4~L5 gaps to the PDE inpainters fall from roughly $7$\,dB to $0.6$\,dB as the budget quadruples. Because the subset and the baseline values come from different samples, these gaps are descriptive rather than paired estimates.

The main results stay at 600 iterations by choice, because a uniform audited budget applied identically to every method and cell avoids making the iteration count a function of the corruption. The statement is therefore twofold: at the audited budget CSFR loses on CF2 and the severe masks, and that loss is recoverable by spending computation, at the runtime cost Table~S2 already identifies as the method's main practical weakness.







\subsection{Downstream Utility: Digit Classification on Corrupted SVHN}
\label{sec:results:downstream}

Reconstruction quality alone does not establish utility, so the methods are also compared on a recognition task. Corrupted SVHN house-number digits are generated with the same mask generator (CF1--CF4, L1--L5, 1000 class-balanced test digits per cell), reconstructed by every method, and classified by a CNN frozen after training on clean images (clean top-1 $0.8783$ on the full SVHN test set, $0.8690$ on the 1000-digit evaluation subset against which accuracy recovery is computed). Table~\ref{tab:downstream} reports mixed results. CSFR recovers accuracy under light corruption but trails LaMa by $5$ to $10$ percentage points in most severe cells. On the joint confident-error rate $P(\text{wrong} \land \text{confidence} \ge 0.9)$ CSFR has the lower rate in several cells. On the conditional rate $P(\text{wrong} \mid \text{confidence} \ge 0.9)$ it leads in one cell (CF3~L4), trails in one (CF2~L5), and is otherwise indistinguishable.

The difference between the two rates is central to the result. The classifier assigns high confidence to CSFR reconstructions far less often than to LaMa's: at CF3~L5 it is confident on 16 of 1000 CSFR outputs against 135 of LaMa's, and the conditional error rates there are $0.250$ and $0.489$. This difference favours CSFR but is not significant at the available sample size. Much of CSFR's joint-rate advantage therefore reflects confidence frequency rather than reliability after confidence is induced. Neither rate is interpretable without the accompanying count $n_{\text{conf}}$, and a low joint rate should not be read as high reliability. The experiment does not establish why the classifier is more conservative on CSFR's outputs; the mechanisms suggested below are hypotheses, and confidence calibration of deep models is itself an open problem~\cite{mehrtash2020confidence}.

The Bounded-Unknown abstention mechanism, intended to flag unrecoverable regions, saturates across cells; Table~S4 reports per-cell rates. The binary flag at the $0.5$ threshold on isolated-missing-fraction carries almost no per-patch information. It is zero for every CF1 cell and for CF3/CF4 at L1, and unity for CF3/CF4 from L2 upward and CF2 from L3 upward; only CF2 at L1 and L2 ($0.88$ and $0.99$) vary within a cell. CF3/CF4 at L1 are the clearest sign that the threshold is the problem: their isolated-missing-fraction is exactly $0.500$, so whether the whole cell flags turns on a strict rather than a non-strict inequality. In effect the flag is a family-and-level indicator, and the continuous isolated-missing-fraction distribution reported beside it is the statistic to use instead.

The signal-covering subset is a separate device, defined on the corruption geometry rather than on constraint violations: it is the set of patches in which at least a quarter of the centre box is erased, so that the corruption actually overlaps the digit. It degenerates at both ends of the severity range. It is empty for CF1 at L1 and L2 and for CF3/CF4 at L1 through L3, where the mask leaves the digit untouched, and it saturates at all 1000 patches for every family from L4 upward. Only four cells carry within-cell variation, CF1~L3 (982) and CF2 at L1, L2 and L3 (352, 760, 991), which is why the bootstrap intervals computed on that subset are informative in the burst family and degenerate elsewhere.



This experiment and the primary sweep of Section~\ref{sec:results:psnr} both ran on an NVIDIA T4, every method within a single job, so no method here was given a separate or more favourable run. The two PyTorch methods used the GPU and the OpenCV and scikit-learn baselines ran on CPU for want of a GPU implementation; the environment is recorded in \texttt{results/\allowbreak downstream/\allowbreak environment.json}. Device bears on runtime, reported separately on a uniform CPU path in Section~\ref{sec:results:cost}, and not on the reconstructions compared here.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} Reconstruction-level PSNR and task-level accuracy measure different outcomes. Under random pixel loss (CF1), all methods recover accuracy near the ceiling, so the results do not identify a preferred method. Under burst and severe structured corruption (CF2--CF4, L3+), LaMa recovers higher accuracy, while the frozen CNN is less often confident on CSFR reconstructions: at CF3~L5 it makes 16 confident predictions of 1000 on CSFR's outputs against 135 on LaMa's. The joint confident-error rate combines reliability with confidence frequency. The conditional rate isolates reliability after confidence is induced; CSFR performs better in CF3~L4, worse in CF2~L5 and shows no advantage in the remaining cells. The joint-rate result therefore partly reflects how often each method induces high confidence. The experiment does not identify the mechanism. Distribution shift and uncertainty signals from CSFR's pixel-domain constraints are possible explanations, but they were not measured. Operational use should consider both confident-error frequency and coverage. Human review remains necessary for outputs tendered as evidence (Section~\ref{sec:discussion:recommendations}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What an Algorithm Can and Cannot Establish}
\label{sec:discussion:admissibility}

Admissibility is a determination made by a court, under a particular jurisdiction's rules, about a particular item of evidence in a particular case. An algorithm cannot possess this property, and no result in this paper claims that CSFR outputs are admissible. The reliability factors enumerated in \emph{Daubert}~\cite{daubert1993}, such as a known error rate, testability, peer review, and governing standards, describe the questions a court may ask. What a method can do is make those questions answerable.

On that narrower claim, CSFR provides exact preservation of accepted observed pixels in the released output, deterministic execution under a recorded environment, measured reconstruction-error distributions (Section~\ref{sec:results}) and approximate sensitivity records for declared queries. The conditional bound of Eq.~\eqref{eq:recovery} applies to the idealised estimator under appropriate RIP assumptions, not to an individual fixed-budget output. Each of these is a precondition for meaningful scrutiny, not a substitute for it. These properties support scrutiny and reproducibility but do not determine admissibility or semantic correctness, and acceptance in the relevant scientific community is, for a method introduced in this paper, not yet established.

Generative inpainters occupy a different position on the same axes. No closed-form error bound is generally available for a sample drawn from a learned prior, and reproducibility depends on model weights and sampling state that the examining laboratory may not control or be able to disclose. These are real obstacles to evidential use. They are not, however, obstacles to every forensic use of such tools; see below.

\subsection{Hallucination Risk Across Investigative, Analytical, and Evidential Use}

The consequences of imputed content depend on what the reconstruction is used for, and collapsing that distinction overstates the case. Three uses can be separated.

In investigative triage, an examiner may use a visually plausible completion to identify fragments for further review. The output is a pointer, not evidence. Generative inpainting may be suitable if its outputs are labelled as generated and kept separate from the evidence record. In analytical use, a reconstruction can guide interpretation without being tendered, but imputed content may bias the examiner. Disclosure and separation of the source and reconstruction can reduce this risk. In evidential use, imputed content presents a more serious problem. A convincing but invented licence-plate digit changes the record from observation to inference. An opposing expert may then argue that the disputed character came from a training distribution rather than the examined disk. Separation matters for a further reason: downstream image-forensic detectors such as camera-model fingerprinting~\cite{cozzolino2020noiseprint} and forgery localisation~\cite{zhuo2022selfadversarial,wu2022robust} operate on pixel-level statistics that any imputation, learned or classical, alters, so such analyses should be run on the source rather than on a reconstructed derivative.

CSFR targets audit-oriented analytical and evidential workflows. It does not sample from a learned prior external to the input, but this restriction does not guarantee that an imputed pixel is correct. An imputed pixel derived entirely from observed neighbours can still be incorrect, and the HRP results of Section~\ref{sec:results:hrp} show that agreement with observed intensity statistics is not proof of semantic accuracy. Source evidence and reconstructed derivatives should therefore be kept clearly separated.

\subsection{What Constraint-Driven Reconstruction Adds}

The operational contribution is the persistence of an approximate sensitivity record for declared CSFR queries, from which a laboratory can answer audit questions about how selected imputed pixels depend on the observed input under the recorded configuration. Comparable queries against a generative model require model-interpretability techniques whose own validity is contested~\cite{bhadra2021hallucinations,horsman2024sources}. Classical interpolation, PDE inpainting and dictionary learning are deterministic and could also be instrumented, although the evaluated implementations did not emit comparable records (Section~\ref{sec:results:ts}). The present study therefore establishes the availability of the CSFR audit artefact, not exclusive traceability of the underlying method.

\subsection{Operational Recommendation}
\label{sec:discussion:recommendations}

A cautious deployment pattern would preserve the source image, store the reconstructed derivative separately, attach the solver configuration and approximate sensitivity record, and require human review when Bounded-Unknown is raised and for any reconstruction tendered evidentially. The downstream experiment of Section~\ref{sec:results:downstream} shows an accuracy--coverage trade-off. CSFR recovers accuracy comparably under light corruption but usually trails LaMa under severe corruption. Its lower joint confident-error rate in some severe cells largely reflects fewer high-confidence predictions; conditional reliability shows no consistent advantage. These findings do not establish that CSFR should replace other methods in operational casework.

\subsection{Adoption Scope and Validation Priorities}
\label{sec:discussion:scope}

CSFR is not proposed as a universal replacement for inpainting methods. The evidence supports adoption in the regime where its measured advantage and design goals coincide: scattered pixel loss and audit-oriented workflows that require deterministic processing, exact preservation of accepted observations and an inspectable record of declared sensitivity queries. For burst corruption and large structured gaps, the evaluated LaMa and PDE implementations often provide higher reconstruction quality. In investigative triage, those alternatives may therefore be preferable, provided generated derivatives are labelled and kept separate from the source evidence.

The auditability claim is deliberately narrower than a claim of operational benefit. This study establishes that the reference implementation can emit reproducible approximate local sensitivity records; it does not establish their stability across perturbation scales, optimisation budgets, hardware or hyperparameter choices, and it does not show that examiners make better decisions when given the records. Direct comparison with SHAP, saliency methods and influence functions also remains open. Those methods were developed primarily to explain predictive-model outputs, whereas the present record approximates local dependence of a reconstruction solution on observed pixels. Uncertainty quantification for learned reconstruction~\cite{edupuganti2021uncertainty,narnhofer2022bayesian,ekmekci2022uncertainty} addresses a related but distinct question, the dispersion of outputs under a learned prior, and offers protocols that future comparisons could adapt. Such comparisons should evaluate repeatability, faithfulness to controlled perturbations and examiner usefulness rather than visual similarity alone.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

Five threats bound what the evidence supports.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{From patches to files.} The evaluation is dominated by synthetic corruption masks on decoded $64\times64$ pixel patches. The three Nick Mikus images provide an illustrative full-resolution case study only; they are too few to establish field performance. Generalisation to naturally damaged fragments, whole encoded files, storage-level fragmentation across non-contiguous clusters, damaged-JPEG recovery and re-synthesis of a valid encoded bitstream remains unvalidated, and is the principal external-validity threat for operational deployment. The protocol is also anchored on JPEG; other container formats are untested.
  \item \textbf{Corpus concentration.} Two of the six DFRWS 2006 source images supply $77\,\%$ of the primary corpus, which leaves cluster-level hypothesis testing uninformative there and is why the external GovDocs1 corpus carries the generalisation claim. Both corpora are document- and photograph-heavy; medical, satellite and low-light surveillance statistics are unmeasured.
  \item \textbf{Downstream scope.} The utility experiment uses one frozen classifier, one corpus, one mask draw per image and one seed. Whether the confident-error behaviour transfers to other architectures or tasks is not established. The mechanism behind the classifier's lower confidence on CSFR reconstructions is likewise unmeasured, and the interpretations offered in Section~\ref{sec:results:downstream} are hypotheses.
  \item \textbf{Configuration bias, across methods and devices.} Results reflect one solver realisation: Adam on the penalised surrogate at a fixed $600$-iteration budget, giving reproducible iterates that are not certified minimisers. Section~\ref{sec:results:budget} indicates that the CF2 and severe-mask deficits are substantially an artefact of that budget. A provably convergent proximal or primal-dual implementation, such as FISTA, ADMM~\cite{chan2017plugandplay} or a primal-dual splitting method matched to the non-smooth terms, has not been evaluated; comparing convergence residuals, runtime and reconstruction quality against Adam is a priority for future work. Baselines run at fixed settings (OpenCV radius $3$; a 64-atom dictionary with 8-sparse OMP), and per-family tuning could move the gaps either way. The primary sweep and the downstream experiment ran on GPU and the external validation and runtime benchmark on CPU, under different dependency versions in each case (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); the quality comparisons are within-job and therefore unaffected, but a cross-corpus comparison of absolute PSNR between Tables~\ref{tab:psnr-ssim} and~\ref{tab:external} spans two environments as well as two corpora.
  \item \textbf{Constraint coverage.} The implemented constraints comprise terminal preservation of observed pixels, total-variation and frequency penalties, and global $[0,255]$ clipping. Field damage such as chemical degradation of photographic film may require additional families. The Bounded-Unknown diagnostic is based on isolated missingness and saturates in many cells (Section~\ref{sec:results:downstream}); it should be recalibrated before operational use. When $\Omega$ is empty or highly sparse, the theoretical RIP condition may fail, but the current implementation does not test the RIP or certify irrecoverability.
\end{enumerate}

Two properties often listed as limitations are reported as findings instead. CSFR's quality deficit outside scattered pixel loss is a measured result (Section~\ref{sec:results:psnr}), not a concession. The solver's $O(T \cdot N \log N)$ cost against a pre-trained inpainter's single forward pass is a runtime result (Section~\ref{sec:results:cost}) that places CSFR in offline analysis rather than real-time triage.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Forensic reconstruction requires a clear distinction between observed and imputed pixels, reproducible processing and explicit limits on what the output establishes. CSFR addresses these needs in the decoded pixel domain through deterministic optimisation, terminal preservation of observed pixels, global range clipping and persisted approximate sensitivity records for sampled imputed pixels. An associated idealised compressed-sensing estimator admits a conditional recovery bound under appropriate RIP assumptions. The fixed-budget implementation does not provide a per-instance recovery certificate.

The evaluation uses inferential results rather than point estimates alone. CSFR's quality advantage is well supported in one regime. Under scattered pixel loss, its lead of about $1.0$ to $1.5$\,dB holds at every severity, survives multiplicity correction and source-image clustering, and reproduces on a separate corpus. The ranking reverses when corruption is spatially concentrated. LaMa records the highest PSNR in 13 of 20 cells, by up to $9.1$\,dB at the audited budget. Section~\ref{sec:results:budget} indicates that fixed-budget under-convergence explains part of this deficit.

Downstream results show an accuracy--coverage trade-off. LaMa recovers more accuracy under severe corruption, while the frozen classifier makes fewer high-confidence predictions on CSFR outputs. Consequently, lower joint confident-error rates do not imply consistently better conditional reliability. The evidence therefore supports CSFR for scattered-loss, audit-oriented reconstruction, not as a general-purpose quality leader. Its empirical contribution is the combination of exact preservation of observed pixels, deterministic processing and an inspectable approximate sensitivity record in the evaluated implementation. The operational value and stability of that record still require controlled perturbation studies and examiner-centred validation. Admissibility remains a determination for a court.

The results support further development of constraint-driven, audit-oriented reconstruction, but they do not establish byte-level file recovery, semantic correctness, exclusive traceability or legal admissibility. Future work should validate storage-level fragment alignment, instrument classical baselines with comparable audit records, repeat downstream testing across seeds and classifiers, and evaluate a solver whose convergence matches the theoretical estimator. Framework, solver, analysis and protocol are released with the paper.
